## MILLWRIGHT  —  52.3/80

*Lens: pve-and-coop*

**Judge means** — depth 8.7 · skill 8.3 · tech 6.3 · chain 6.0 · econ 5.3 · cold start 6.7 · ship 6.0 · originality 5.0

- [advantage-player] The strongest economic model in the set: bots are the content pipeline, not a threat, revenue is subscription plus module royalties rather than adversarial wagering, and the Zachtronics genre has a decade-proven long tail.
- [solo-shipper] The best-engineered MVP in all 45 concepts -- Zachtronics-proven fun, a real verifiable-leaderboard case for the chain, works solo at N=1, and the tx-chunked CU budget shows someone actually did the math.
- [strategy-player] This is Opus Magnum with a verifiable leaderboard and a real module economy — one of the deepest, most proven genres in the medium — and it barely needs other players online to be worth playing.

**Pitch.** A weekly engineering puzzle where you build a machine on a grid, the chain runs it and stamps the exact cycle count nobody can fake — and if your machine is good, other players pay to bolt a sealed copy of it into theirs.

**Core loop.** Every Monday one Contract is published: a 24x24 tile grid, a fixed input feed, and an output spec (e.g. 'produce 60 Type-C assemblies'). You place components from a palette of 14 (belt, splitter, merger, stamper, buffer, comparator gate, arm, sensor, welder, sorter, clock, latch, crusher, vent), each with rotation and a 1-byte parameter. You press Verify; the on-chain VM executes your blueprint for up to 4,000 deterministic ticks and records three numbers: CYCLES (ticks to spec), FOOTPRINT (bounding-box tiles), COST (sum of component prices). There is no single winner — there are three leaderboards plus a Pareto frontier, exactly like Opus Magnum. A first working solution takes 20-40 minutes. Getting from 'works' to top-quartile takes 3-6 more iterations of 10-15 minutes each, which is where the game actually lives. Typical engaged player: 5-9 verified runs per contract, ~2.5 hours across a week, returning Mon/Wed/Sat. Every verification is public and replayable frame-by-frame, so the leaderboard is not a claim, it is a receipt. Second layer: any verified sub-assembly you own can be sealed as a MODULE — a rectangle with a published I/O contract and verified throughput numbers but hidden internals. Other players drop it into their grid as a black box and pay you 0.002 SOL each time they verify a run containing it. So the metagame is: solve the contract, then notice that your 6x4 buffer-sorter is better than anyone else's and start renting it out.

**Depth curve.** Optimization games have the best-documented long tail in the medium — Zachtronics leaderboards were still moving years after launch, and Factorio's D365 is a genre outlier — because the score is continuous, not binary. Day 1 you are asking 'does this work'. Day 100 you are fighting for a 3% cycle reduction by overlapping two arms on a shared clock phase, and you are reading other people's UNSEALED historical solutions to steal ideas. Three distinct mastery tracks compound: (1) component-level tricks (clock-phase packing, using a comparator as a one-tick delay), (2) global architecture (parallel lanes vs. a single fast pipeline — the right answer flips depending on whether the contract's bottleneck is input rate or output ordering), (3) module economics — pricing a module too high means nobody licenses it, too low means you subsidize your competitors' scores. Every archived contract stays permanently open with its own ladder, so a day-100 player has ~14 back-contracts to grind plus the live one. And the module market means the content the developer has to author is a 200-byte contract spec per week, while the actual difficulty is authored by other players. The honest weakness: this is deep but narrow. It retains people who like this exact thing extremely well, and everyone else bounces in ten minutes. That is a market-size problem, not a retention problem.

**Why on-chain.** Three things break on a normal server. First, the score is only worth chasing if it cannot be faked, and 'we ran your machine on our server, trust us' is exactly the claim that makes leaderboard grinding feel pointless — running the VM as a public program means every score has an executable proof anyone can re-run and every replay is deterministic from a 288-byte blueprint. Second, the module royalty. The entire pitch to an author is 'the platform cannot delist you, cannot stop paying you, and cannot change your split' — a Token-2022 transfer-fee/hook-enforced royalty is a promise a web platform structurally cannot make (see: every UGC platform that changed its revenue share). Third, the weekly contract seed is drawn by Switchboard On-Demand VRF with the proof published before anyone plays, so the developer provably did not author a contract to suit a friend's known technique. The honest concession: the SIMULATION itself does not need a chain — I could run it in a browser. What needs the chain is the settlement of scores and the unrevocable payment of authors.

**Money flow.** IN, three streams. (1) Foundry Pass, 0.06 SOL/month (~$5): gates module PUBLISHING, the 14 archived back-contract ladders, the replay-diff tool, and the private sandbox. It explicitly does NOT gate verification runs on the live contract — everyone gets unlimited verified runs, free, forever. (2) Module licensing: author sets the fee, default 0.002 SOL (~$0.16) per verified run containing the module; 90% to author, 10% to the developer, split atomically by the program. (3) Commission board: a player or guild posts a bounty in USDC for a solution to a custom contract they authored ('$40 for anyone who gets this under 900 cycles by Friday'); entry is free, the developer takes 5% of the bounty on payout. SINKS: the Pass is consumed monthly; the license fee is consumed per run and is not recoverable. Nothing accrues, nothing yields, there is no pool that pays returns to depositors, so there is no ponzi geometry available — the worst case is the game gets boring and people stop paying $5. ARITHMETIC at realistic scale: 2,000 registered, 250 Pass holders = $1,250/mo. Module volume: 400 players averaging 6 verified runs/week with 1.4 licensed modules each = 3,360 license events/week at $0.16 = $538/week gross, $54/week to the developer. Commission board at 30 bounties/month averaging $35 = $1,050 volume, $52 to the developer. Developer total ~$1,520/mo; top module author earning ~$120/mo, small but real and paid instantly to a wallet anywhere on earth. Weekly bounty prizes (1.0/0.6/0.4 SOL to the top three on each axis) are funded by a published rule — 20% of the PRIOR month's Pass revenue, never from entrant money — so the prize pool physically cannot outrun revenue.

**Legal structure (as generated).** Tier 0, with a free-entry skill-contest layer on top. CONSIDERATION IS REMOVED from every competitive path: entry to the weekly contract, unlimited verification, and eligibility for the developer-funded bounty are all free and require no purchase. The Foundry Pass buys authoring tools and archived content, not competitive access or competitive advantage on the live ladder — this distinction is load-bearing and must be enforced in code, not marketing copy, because regulators assess what the product functionally is. CHANCE IS REMOVED ENTIRELY: the VM is deterministic, the contract seed is drawn by VRF but is symmetric (bit-identical for every player, published before play), and there is no per-player randomness anywhere — no drop tables, no crits, no variance. With zero chance in the outcome, even the harsh MATERIAL ELEMENT test has nothing to bite on, and Florida's statutory bar on wagering on skill games is not engaged because there is no wager. The remaining exposures are ordinary commerce questions, not gambling ones: (a) the module marketplace is a software licensing marketplace — the program is non-custodial, funds route atomically from licensee to author with the developer never taking custody, which keeps the money-transmitter analysis in the FinCEN 2019 non-custodial-software lane rather than the MSB lane; (b) the commission board is a services marketplace, same as Upwork, with a non-custodial escrow; (c) UK — nothing here is 'money or money's worth' won by chance, and the tradable module NFT is a licence sold by its author, not a prize. No sweepstakes structure, no dual currency, no offshore licence, no prediction contracts — none of the Tier 2 through Tier 5 landmines are touched. Add plain terms of service and a 1099 process for authors over $600/yr; no state-by-state geofence is required.

**Bots.** Bots are not a threat here, they are the content pipeline, and the design says so out loud. Day one an automated search program (SAT solver, beam search, RL) will beat every human on the CYCLES axis of a simple contract, because that is what optimization games are — the Zachtronics community has run 'unbounded' solver divisions for a decade. Rather than pretend to detect them, MILLWRIGHT declares two ladders sharing one verification path: HAND (self-declared, unenforced, the social ladder people actually care about) and OPEN (anything goes, solver-authored solutions welcome, where the record lives). Crucially the economics absorb the bot: a solver that finds a superb 6x4 sorter cannot cash out by winning a purse (weekly bounties total ~$80 split three ways), it cashes out by SEALING that sorter as a module and renting it — so the bot operator's incentive is to publish useful components into the ecosystem and everyone else's game gets better. Multi-accounting gains nothing: there is no per-account payout, and a sybil cannot inflate module revenue without paying the license fee to itself minus a 10% developer cut, a net loss of real SOL per wash. The one genuine abuse is a solver farming the commission board to snipe every bounty; mitigation is that bounty posters pick the winner from a shortlist and can specify HAND-only, and repeat sniping just means the poster stops posting, which is self-correcting.

**First session.** 0:00 — a friend posts a replay link in Discord: a 41-second animated GIF of a machine, captioned '812 cycles, beat it'. You click. 0:10 — the browser shows the replay playing on a CSS grid, and below it the live leaderboard for Contract #17 with 63 entries and the VRF proof for the seed. There is a PLAY button. No wallet prompt. 0:20 — you are in the editor with the same contract, dragging belts. All client-side; you have spent nothing and connected nothing. 4:00 — you have a machine you think works. You hit SIMULATE; the local WASM copy of the VM (bit-identical to the on-chain program) runs it. It jams at tick 340. 14:00 — it works, 2,140 cycles. Now the button says VERIFY ON-CHAIN, and only now does it ask for a wallet. 14:30 — connect Phantom/Backpack. You have 0 SOL. The page shows 'You need ~0.003 SOL (~$0.25) to publish scores for a month. Get it in 30 seconds' with a MoonPay/Coinbase Onramp widget and a receive QR. Alternatively — and this is the path most first sessions take — a FIRST FORGE faucet: the program funds any new wallet that submits a valid working solution to the tutorial contract with 0.004 SOL, once per solution-hash, so your first real verification is paid for by the act of solving something. 16:00 — five signed transactions land (200-tick chunks, ~25,000 lamports total); a progress bar shows chunk 3 of 5. The landing layer is doing real work: Helius Sender with a staked connection, fresh blockhash per chunk, CU limit set to measured usage plus 10%, retry with re-fetched blockhash on failure. 16:40 — your score posts. You are 47th of 64. Your replay link is generated. 17:00 — you open the #1 entry's replay, see they used a comparator as a delay line, mutter something, and reopen the editor.

**Viral mechanic.** The replay link IS the advertising, and it costs the developer nothing. Every verified run generates a permanent URL rendering the machine animating on a CSS/canvas grid with the score stamped on it, plus a one-click 'same contract, your turn' button dropping the viewer into the editor. That is a challenge object legible to a non-player in four seconds — you can see the machine is clever without understanding the rules, the same reason Factorio clips travel. Second vector: the Pareto frontier chart is genuinely beautiful and shareable, and sole occupancy of a corner ('nobody has ever done it in under 40 tiles') is a brag with an on-chain receipt. Third: module authorship creates a public credit line — 'this solution uses M-Ring by @author' appears on every replay licensing it, so top authors get attributed advertising on other people's viral posts. Distributed as a Solana Blink for the challenge link and as a normal web app; Solana Mobile dApp Store as a launch beat, not a growth plan.

**Four-week MVP.** SHIP: 12x12 grid, 6 components (belt, splitter, merger, stamper, buffer, gate), 1,000-tick cap, 288-byte blueprints in a single 1,232-byte L1 transaction, sim chunked 200 ticks/instruction across 5 transactions, one score PDA per player-contract, three-axis leaderboard, permanent replay links, a WASM build of the identical VM for instant local simulation, 4 hand-authored contracts plus 1 tutorial, the First Forge faucet, and the Foundry Pass as a simple monthly PDA subscription. Rendering is a CSS grid with unicode glyphs — zero art budget, and it reads deliberately as an engineering schematic. Week 1: VM in Rust + WASM parity harness (the parity test suite is the most important code in the project and is written first). Week 2: Anchor program, chunked verification, score PDAs. Week 3: editor UI, replay renderer, landing layer with Helius Sender. Week 4: contracts, Pass, faucet, deploy, and seed the ladder by playing it myself for a week before opening. EXPLICITLY CUT: modules and licensing entirely (v2, weeks 6-10 — this is the whole Private-ER/TEE piece and must not be in the MVP), the commission board (v2), the ephemeral rollup and 24x24 grids (v2), VRF seeding (v1 uses a pre-committed hashed seed list published at launch, honest and free), historical ladders, mobile layout. If week 3 slips, cut the Pass and ship free — the ladder is the product; monetization can arrive in week 6.

**Biggest risk.** Audience, not design. This is a Zachtronics-shaped game and the addressable Solana population is ~3.2M daily wallets overwhelmingly present to trade, not to spend 40 minutes optimizing a conveyor belt. The acquisition contradiction bites in its purest form: the channels reaching actual puzzle-game players (Steam, r/incremental_games, Hacker News) are where mentioning crypto is a liability or an outright ban, and the channels where crypto is welcome deliver people who bounce in ninety seconds because there is nothing to speculate on. Mitigation is to lean all the way into invisible-crypto positioning — market it as a free engineering puzzle with a leaderboard nobody can fake, never say 'web3', let the wallet appear only at the verify step, and accept revenue of $1-2k/month from a few hundred devoted people rather than a spike. Secondary risk: the module market never reaches liquidity — below ~50 published modules nobody licenses anything and the 'creators earn' pillar is decorative, which is precisely why it is cut from the MVP rather than being the MVP.

**Solana primitives.**
  - Anchor program implementing a deterministic tick VM; simulation chunked at 200 ticks/instruction (~1.15M CU against the 1,400,000 CU per-transaction cap, well clear of the 200,000 CU per-instruction default which is raised via ComputeBudget)
  - One score PDA per (player, contract) — per-player writable accounts, so the 12M CU per writable account per block ceiling is never approached and local fee markets keep priority fees at ~0
  - MVP blueprint encoding at 288 bytes (12x12 grid, 2 bytes/cell) fits inside the live 1,232-byte transaction limit; SIMD-0296's 4,096-byte limit is NOT live so v1 is designed under 1,232
  - MagicBlock Ephemeral Rollup at v2 for the full 24x24 grid: 1,152-byte blueprints plus verification traces exceed L1 tx size but sit trivially inside the ER's 64 KB transaction limit; 4,000 ticks stream across ~14 free ER transactions (0 SOL base fee), final score committed to L1 at 0.0001 SOL, session undelegated at 0.0003 SOL — total ~$0.03 per fully-verified run
  - MagicBlock Private ER (TEE-backed) for sealed MODULES: the module's internals execute inside the TEE so a licensee gets verified throughput numbers without ever seeing the blueprint — the only way module licensing has value, since all L1 account data is public
  - Token-2022 transfer-fee extension on module license NFTs so the 10% platform cut and the author split are enforced at the token-program level, not by an off-chain honour system
  - Switchboard On-Demand VRF for the weekly contract seed, proof posted on-chain before the contract opens — symmetric randomness (every player gets the bit-identical contract), which keeps the whole product inside pure-skill analysis
  - Rent-exempt run PDAs (~6,960 lamports/byte) explicitly closed and refunded after archival; a 2 KB scratch account for inter-chunk sim state costs ~0.014 SOL and is fully recovered

### Hostile review

*backfilled 2026-08-21; reviews the raw concept, not the later build spec*

- **Verdict:** buildable-with-caveats
- **Solo-dev estimate:** 6 weeks
- **Degenerate strategy:** The leaderboard rewards a score, not authorship, and every verified run is a public, frame-by-frame replay — which means the exact blueprint (component types, positions, rotations on a small, fixed-palette grid) of the #1 solution is directly recoverable by watching the replay. There is no on-chain check for duplicate or near-duplicate blueprint submission. So the metagame collapses to: wait for the top replay (posted fast, since a solver bot wins day one per the design's own botStory), decode it, resubmit the identical blueprint under your own wallet, claim the same score. Once that's common knowledge — and the viral mechanic actively pushes people to watch others' replays — 'design your own machine' stops being the fastest path to rank, undercutting the retention loop the whole depth curve depends on.
- **Technical blockers:**
  - The chunk-count arithmetic in solanaPrimitives doesn't hold: v2 claims '4,000 ticks stream across ~14 free ER transactions,' but the same list gives ~1.15M CU per 200-tick chunk against the hard 1,400,000 CU per-transaction ceiling. Two chunks in one transaction is 2.3M CU, over the cap, so each chunk needs its own transaction: 4,000/200 = 20 chunks, not 14. The dollar-cost claim survives (MagicBlock ER pricing is per-session/per-commit, not per-transaction) but the number is simply wrong as stated, and it means a full 24x24 verify is 20 sequential, state-dependent, must-land-in-order transactions rather than 14.
  - The chunk chain has no specified resume logic. Each 200-tick chunk's input is the prior chunk's ending state; a dropped transaction mid-chain (the primitives doc's own 'five compounding failure modes': stale blockhash, undersized CU, mispriced fee, late packet, blind retry) forces either a full restart from tick 0 or an unbuilt checkpoint mechanism. Nothing in fourWeekMvp addresses this, so a new player's very first verification — the one funded by the First Forge faucet, the whole onboarding hook — can silently die on chunk 3 of 5 with the fee spent and zero score shown.
  - Hitting ~5,750 CU/tick (1.15M/200) for full-grid mutation requires zero-copy account access (Pinocchio-style raw byte-slice mutation, not Borsh (de)serialization inside the tick loop); the doc specifies 'Anchor program' with no mention of zero-copy. A naive Anchor implementation that (de)serializes the grid struct on every tick rather than once per instruction will blow past the 1,400,000 CU/tx ceiling well before 200 ticks. Solvable, but currently unaddressed, and exactly the kind of gap that eats week 3-4 of a 4-week MVP.
- **Economic holes:**
  - Module-market cold start is likely worse than the doc's own admission. Publishing is gated behind the $5/mo Foundry Pass (250 projected holders), and only a top-quartile sub-assembly is rentable at $0.16/run. The doc's own arithmetic needs 'below ~50 published modules nobody licenses anything,' but reaching 50 genuinely SOTA modules realistically takes months of iteration by a small paying subset of players — during which the 'creators earn' pillar, the concept's actual differentiator over a free browser Zachtronics clone, earns close to nothing.
  - A first-mover solver bot captures a compounding annuity, not a one-time prize. A sealed module keeps earning 90% of every license fee for as long as it stays best-in-class for its I/O contract, so the first well-resourced SAT/RL solver to publish a top module doesn't just win once — it taxes every later licensee indefinitely until displaced, converting 'creators earn' into 'the first bot to arrive rents to everyone after it.'
- **Cold start:** Degrades gracefully, which is the design's real strength: at 8 concurrent players the core loop (build, simulate free in local WASM, verify on-chain) works identically to 8,000 players, since there's no matchmaking or shared mutable state. What breaks is everything layered on top — an 8-entry leaderboard isn't a flex, replay links need an audience to be worth sharing, and the Foundry Pass / module market don't reach revenue-relevant scale (8 players at even 50% conversion is ~$20/month, and a module market needs dozens of published modules plus buyers on both sides before any liquidity exists). Solo value survives; two-sided-marketplace value does not exist below roughly the low hundreds of engaged players.
- **Bot extraction:** A single operator running a SAT/ILP or RL solver (the exact tooling the Zachtronics and gas-golfing communities have run against this genre for a decade) can plausibly clear every axis of a week's contract before most humans finish a first working solution. Extraction is two-layered and recurring: (1) sweeping the weekly bounty pool (1.0/0.6/0.4 SOL ≈ $80/$48/$32 at SOL≈$80) across all three axes ≈ $550-690/month, essentially risk-free once the solver exists; (2) once module licensing ships, the same solver's best sub-assemblies become sealed modules earning 90% of $0.16-per-run license fees indefinitely as long as they stay best-in-class — a recurring annuity stacked on top of the weekly sweep.
- **Prior art:** Primodium (Ethereum, MUD framework) is the closest shipped analogue — a fully on-chain, Factorio-style factory/production-chain game, mainnet, multiple public playtests since 2023, a major version overhaul (v0.7) — and despite real backing it has never demonstrated a mainstream audience, exactly the 'deep but narrow' outcome the concept predicts for itself. More directly on-point: on-chain gas-golfing leaderboards (the 2018 Solidity Gas Golfing Contest; Optimizor Club / the IAmTheOptimizor challenge, which mints a fresh NFT to whoever submits a cheaper verified solution) are essentially MILLWRIGHT's exact mechanic — verified, unfakeable, on-chain optimization score with a leaderboard reward — already shipped, years old, and still a tiny niche of researchers and specialists, not a consumer game. No prior art was found for the sealed-module rental/royalty layer specifically; that piece appears genuinely novel and untested.
- **Suggested fix:** Store a hash of each submitted blueprint per contract and reject (or exclude from HAND-ladder ranking) any submission whose canonicalized blueprint hash exactly or near-exactly matches a prior submission from a different wallet. Cheap (one extra account lookup and hash compare), fully on-chain enforceable, and it closes the replay-decode-and-resubmit hole without touching the OPEN ladder, which already welcomes solver-authored and copied solutions.

## LOCKMAKER  —  50.0/80

*Lens: unused-primitives*

**Judge means** — depth 8.0 · skill 7.3 · tech 6.7 · chain 5.7 · econ 5.3 · cold start 4.3 · ship 6.0 · originality 6.7

- [advantage-player] Smart bot-tolerant design (attempts cost money whether or not the solver succeeds, so bots are paying customers), but it's a two-sided marketplace with a classic cold-start problem: nobody authors locks when there's no audience yet.
- [solo-shipper] TIS-100-as-a-marketplace with ancestry royalties is a genuinely great, well-scoped idea -- the UGC chicken-and-egg problem (nobody authors without an audience) is the honest risk, and it's the right one to worry about.
- [strategy-player] TIS-100-style signal-routing golf with an ancestry-royalty marketplace on top is genuinely deep puzzle design; the marketplace needs authors and authors need an audience that doesn't exist yet.

**Pitch.** A puzzle game where players build tiny signal-routing locks and everyone else pays a few cents to try to pick them — and the person who designed the lock gets paid every time, forever, including a cut to whoever's lock they remixed.

**Core loop.** Two loops, both ~30 seconds.

PICKING (the consumer loop): You open a lock — a 9x9 grid of 12 component types (deflector, gate, counter, splitter, mirror, delay, sentinel, plate, one-way, decoy, timer, sink), fully visible, zero hidden information, zero randomness. You write a PICK: an ordered program of up to 24 instructions from an 8-verb ISA (PUSH, PULL, CUT, WAIT, SPLIT, PROBE, INVERT, JMPIF), each with 1-2 args, ~64 bytes total. Press run: the deterministic sim ticks up to 240 steps at 60fps in the browser (Rust compiled to wasm — the identical crate compiled to BPF is the on-chain verifier). Signal reaches the core = picked. Cost: 0.001 SOL (~$0.08) per attempt, bought as a 50-attempt keyring for 0.05 SOL in one transaction. Median attempt-to-result: 4 seconds. First-solve is only half the game — every lock keeps a permanent three-column efficiency leaderboard (fewest instructions / fewest ticks / fewest components touched), TIS-100 style, so a solved lock stays competitive forever.

AUTHORING (the producer loop): Same editor, 120-point component budget, costs 5-25 points per piece. You must submit a working pick of your own lock (proves solvability, prevents unsolvable spam) and pay 0.01 SOL to publish. Then it sits in the catalogue earning. Publishing takes 5-20 minutes for a considered lock.

Session: 15-25 minutes, 8-15 attempts across 3-4 locks, or one authoring session.

**Depth curve.** Day 1 you are learning what a splitter does. Day 100 three separate curves are still climbing.

(1) Instruction-golf. Every lock ever published keeps a live efficiency leaderboard. Solving is the tutorial; the sport is solving in 9 instructions instead of 14. This is the TIS-100/Zachtronics histogram effect and it has kept those communities alive for a decade with no new content shipped. A solved lock does not die.

(2) Component-interaction canon. Twelve components with a signal-propagation model produce named tech — the community will discover and name things like 'delay-mirror ratchets' and 'decoy-sentinel starvation', exactly as fighting-game and speedrun communities do. Day-100 players read a lock and see the archetype; day-1 players see a grid.

(3) Composition as a craft with a scoreboard denominated in money. A lock's revenue is a public number. Authors iterate toward the shape that maximises attempts-before-solve, and that is a genuinely open design problem: too hard, nobody plays; too easy, no reputation. The best authors will run 5-lock series with escalating teaching, and the fork tree makes lineage visible.

The honest caveat: curve (3) only exists if authoring reaches critical mass. UGC platforms convert ~1% of users to creators. If it stalls, this degrades to a solid single-player Zachtronics clone with a leaderboard — which is still fun on day 100, just not growing.

**Why on-chain.** The puzzle game runs fine on a normal web server. What does not run on a server is the promise. Two specific things break:

(1) Enforced ancestry royalties across venues the operator does not control. A lock with a public revenue stream is an asset people will sell. On Solana today Metaplex royalties are voluntary — marketplaces stripped them in 2023-24. A Token-2022 transfer hook is the only mechanism that makes a resale physically fail unless the ancestry got paid: the hook program runs inside the transfer, checks for a royalty-receipt PDA created in the same transaction, and reverts if it is absent. Magic Eden, Tensor, a bilateral OTC swap, a future marketplace nobody has written yet — all of them pay, because the mint itself refuses to move otherwise. No Web2 UGC platform (Roblox, Mario Maker, Fortnite Creative) can make this promise, because in every one of them the payout is a policy the publisher can change and the account is a thing the publisher can delete.

(2) The hook is also a rules engine, not just a toll. It rejects transfers of a lock while that lock is inside an active tournament bracket or has an unsettled vault — which prevents the obvious exploit of dumping a lock mid-event onto a buyer who inherits the liability.

If you strip the transfer hook you can still run the game, but the creator pitch collapses to 'trust me', and that pitch is available for free from Roblox with 100x the traffic.

**Money flow.** Value enters exactly one way: a player buys attempts. 0.001 SOL each, sold in 50-packs at 0.05 SOL (~$4 at SOL $80).

Per attempt the split is: author 60%, ancestry chain 15% (8/4/2/1 across up to 4 generations; unfilled generations roll to the author), house 20%, curation purse 5%. The curation purse pays weekly to the players whose early ratings best predicted a lock's eventual attempt volume — this is the discovery subsidy, and it is a real job with real pay.

The sinks: publishing costs 0.01 SOL (burned to the house — this is what stops catalogue spam), and attempts are pure consumption. Nothing here pays out to new entrants from other entrants' deposits. There is no token, no emission, no staking yield. A lock's income is a function of how many people chose to play it, which is the only sustainable shape.

Arithmetic at three scales. 8 players (week 1): ~40 locks (I author them), maybe 600 attempts total = 0.6 SOL gross, house take 0.12 SOL. This is a rounding error and that is fine — week 1 is about whether anyone opens a second lock. 300 monthly actives averaging 90 attempts: 27,000 attempts = 27 SOL/month gross = ~$2,160; house 5.4 SOL (~$430/mo); the top author with 4,000 attempts on their catalogue clears 2.4 SOL (~$190/mo). 3,000 monthly actives: ~$21,600 gross, ~$4,300/mo to the house, top authors at $1,500-2,000/mo — which is the number at which authoring becomes a real side income and the flywheel is self-sustaining.

An important invariant: an author cannot farm their own lock. Self-attempting returns 60% of a 100% cost. Sybil authoring is strictly loss-making. That single number kills the entire farm-your-own-content attack class without any detection heuristics.

Second revenue line: lock resales. A lock with $190/mo of income is a cashflow asset and will trade at some multiple. Every resale pays 8/4/2/1 up the fork chain plus a 2.5% house cut, enforced by the transfer hook. At 300 actives this is small; at scale it is the line that grows fastest.

**Legal structure (as generated).** Tier 0, with one leg of the three-part test removed outright and a second one absent by construction. This is the safest of the three concepts and it is safe by design, not by labelling.

CHANCE: there is none. Not 'skill predominates' — literally none. The sim is deterministic, all information is public before you spend, and the same input produces the same output every time, verifiably, because the verifier is on-chain and open source. This clears the predominance test, the material-element test (~30 states, the harsh one), and even the any-chance test. The reason concept 1 contains no randomness anywhere is precisely this: the moment I add a random element I am arguing about it in 30 states.

PRIZE: there is none in the picking loop. You pay 0.001 SOL to play a puzzle. You cannot win money by picking a lock; you win a leaderboard rank. Money flows one direction — consumer to producer — like buying a level pack, a chess puzzle subscription, or a song. Authors earn revenue from selling access to content they made; that is a marketplace, not a contest.

Bounty locks (the promotion feature): an author escrows SOL, first picker takes it. That has a prize, so consideration must go. Bounty attempts are FREE — no keyring debit, no operator-paid fee, capped at 6 attempts per wallet per lock per day, and the only cost is the 5,000-lamport network fee paid to validators, not to me. Author-funded promotion with free entry and zero chance is a skill contest with no consideration to the promoter. I would still geofence Florida (statutory bar on wagering on skill games) out of caution, and I would not run bounties at all in month one.

UK: locks are tradable items with money's worth, but acquisition involves no chance whatsoever — you buy a specific, fully-inspectable lock at a known price. This is a marketplace listing, not a loot box, and it does not touch the exemption that loot boxes strain.

The real legal exposure here is not gambling, it is SECURITIES, and I want to name it rather than hide it. A lock NFT with a passive income stream, resold to a buyer expecting income generated by a platform I operate, is uncomfortably close to Howey. Mitigations: no pre-sale of any kind, no roadmap, no pooling of proceeds, no promise or projection of appreciation, no operator market-making, and revenue that is strictly usage-based (a lock earns because humans chose to play it — closer to a domain name or a song catalogue than to a share). Marketing copy never mentions lock resale value. If the resale market gets large I take counsel before it does, not after.

**Bots.** Bots are the customers, and this is the concept's best structural joke.

Day one, someone points a SAT solver or a beam search at the 8-verb ISA. Search space for a 24-slot program with args is astronomical, but good heuristic search will crack a meaningful fraction of locks. In any wagering game that is fatal. Here it is revenue: every attempt costs 0.001 SOL, so a bot grinding 10,000 attempts pays 10 SOL and hands 6 of it to the author. A picking bot is a whale who never complains about the UX.

What the bot can extract: leaderboard positions on the efficiency ladder. That is real, and rather than fight it I split the ladder into an OPEN division (anything goes, bots explicitly welcome, this is where the Robocode/Screeps culture lives) and a first-24-hours HUMAN WINDOW where an attempt is only ranked if it came from a wallet holding a Solana Attestation Service proof-of-personhood attestation from a third-party issuer (Civic et al.). No KYC infrastructure of my own; I read an attestation someone else issued. If the attestation ecosystem is too thin to rely on, the human window simply does not exist and the game is still fine — which is the test of whether a mitigation is load-bearing.

What a bot cannot do: farm money out of the system. Self-attempting your own lock returns 60 cents on the dollar. Mass-publishing generated locks costs 0.01 SOL each and earns nothing unless humans choose to play them, and choice is the one thing you cannot automate on the demand side. There is no faucet, no emission, no reward for volume. The bot's only path to profit is picking a bounty, and bounties are author-funded advertising budgets that are capped and optional.

The deeper point: this game has no extractable pool. Bots are dangerous when money pools; here money moves point-to-point from a consumer who chose to spend to a producer who made something. There is no pool to drain.

**First session.** Minute 0. A Blink for a specific lock appears in a Discord or on X — 'THE BRASS ORCHID · 4,182 attempts · 0 picks'. Click through to the web app. No wallet connect prompt yet.

Minute 0-1. The lock renders immediately: 9x9 CSS grid, components as styled divs with emoji glyphs, no image assets anywhere in the product. A 40-second animated demo pick runs on loop showing the ISA in action. There is a big PICK IT button.

Minute 1. Click PICK IT. Now an embedded wallet is created behind a passkey (Face ID / Touch ID) — no seed phrase, no extension, no app store. The user's mental model is 'I made an account'. Their first 20 attempts are gasless: Kora sponsors the 5,000-lamport signature. They have not spent a cent and do not know what a lamport is.

Minute 1-6. They fail four times at THE BRASS ORCHID, which is deliberate — the Blink-linked locks are hard showpieces. A 'try the easy ladder' button routes them to the 12-lock tutorial series I authored. They solve locks 1 through 5 in about five minutes. Each solve shows their instruction count against the global histogram: 'you: 17 instructions. Best: 8.' That histogram is the hook, not the money.

Minute 6-12. They re-solve lock 3 twice trying to beat 11. They get to 12. This is the moment the game either works or does not, and it involves no crypto at all.

Minute 13. Attempt 20. The gasless allowance ends and a funding sheet appears: 'Keyring — 50 attempts — 0.05 SOL (~$4)', with three buttons: pay from an existing Solana wallet (Phantom/Backpack/Solflare via wallet-standard), buy with a card (on-ramp widget, ~90 seconds, the honest bottleneck), or keep playing free in unranked mode with a 6-attempts-per-hour cap. That third option matters: nobody hits a hard paywall, they hit a throttle. Card conversion will be bad — 10-20% — and the free throttle is what keeps the other 80% in the game long enough to convert later.

Minute 20-25. They open the editor 'just to look'. The editor is the same grid with a palette. They build something bad, fail to solve it themselves, and leave. Roughly 1 in 15 will come back and publish within a week; that number is the whole business.

**Viral mechanic.** Three, in order of strength.

(1) The unpicked lock. Every lock's page shows attempts and picks. '6,204 attempts. 0 picks.' is the most shareable object the game produces, and the person sharing it is the author bragging about something they MADE — which is a socially clean brag that survives outside crypto spaces, unlike 'I won 4 SOL'. As soon as someone picks it, the picker has a matching brag ('picked THE BRASS ORCHID in 11') and shares it too. The mechanic is adversarial in a way that guarantees both sides post.

(2) The fork tree. Every lock's page renders its ancestry and its descendants as a visible tree. Forking is one click and inherits the 8/4/2/1 royalty. When someone forks your lock you get a notification with the number attached: 'ORCHID II earned you 0.31 SOL this week'. Being remixed is both flattering and paid, which is the strongest possible incentive to tell people about a platform.

(3) The efficiency histogram screenshot. TIS-100's histogram is one of the most-screenshotted UI elements in indie games. It is free to build (CSS bars) and it makes an ordinary solve postable.

Distribution channels honestly assessed: Blinks are a sharing primitive inside crypto-native feeds, not an acquisition channel — X never natively rendered them and mobile support is weak, so I treat a Blink as a nicer link, nothing more. The real target audience is Zachtronics/puzzle/programming-game players (r/tis100, Hacker News, itch.io, Lobsters), where the crypto element must be entirely invisible and optional in the pitch — the pitch is 'a programming puzzle game where the puzzles are made by players and the makers get paid'. That audience is not the 3.2M daily Solana wallets, and reaching it means never using the word 'earn' in a headline. That is the acquisition contradiction from the brief, and my chosen side of it is: reach actual gamers, never talk about earning.

UGC platforms convert about 1% of players into creators, and that 1% needs an audience to be worth serving. With 8 players in week one there is no audience, so the economic argument for authoring is zero and the only motivation is craft. My 40 seed locks buy maybe three weeks of content. **Solana primitives.**
  - Token-2022 transfer hook — load-bearing. Each published lock is a Token-2022 NFT whose hook program (a) reverts any transfer lacking a same-transaction royalty-receipt PDA proving ancestry was paid, and (b) reverts transfers of locks with open vaults or live tournament entries.
  - CPI depth 4 — the ancestry royalty split is capped at exactly 4 generations (8%/4%/2%/1%) because that is what fits inside the CPI depth limit without a crank. The design constraint became the design.
  - Per-player PDA sharding — attempts write to the attempting player's own PDA. Fee markets are local to writable accounts, so a player attempting a wildly popular lock pays near-zero priority fee. Author revenue accrues into 16 shard vault PDAs per lock (hash of payer % 16) specifically to stay under the 12M CU-per-writable-account-per-block ceiling; the author's claim instruction sweeps all 16 in one tx (well inside the 64 account-lock limit).
  - On-chain deterministic verifier — the same Rust sim crate compiled to BPF. A 9x9 u8 grid x 240 ticks benchmarks at roughly 60-110k CU, inside the 200k default per-instruction budget, so no ComputeBudget over-request (priority fee scales with CU requested, not consumed).
  - Kora fee relayer — sponsors the 5,000-lamport signature fee for a new player's first 20 attempts, so they play before they fund.
  - Blinks/Actions — every lock has a Blink URL; a lock is a shareable object, not a screenshot.
  - Explicitly NOT used: no ephemeral rollup (turn-based, no tick loop to host), no VRF (the game contains zero randomness by design, which is also the legal spine).

## DOCTRINE  —  48.7/80

*Lens: wagered-strategy-a*

**Judge means** — depth 7.3 · skill 7.3 · tech 6.3 · chain 5.7 · econ 5.0 · cold start 4.7 · ship 5.7 · originality 6.7

- [advantage-player] Best-designed anti-solve mechanism in the set: the field is hidden until the daily deadline and shifts every day, so there's no fixed strategy to converge on, and bots are the intended, non-adversarial audience.
- [solo-shipper] Async doctrine-authoring tournament where a player IS a file, not a body in a chair -- one of the best cold-start answers in the set, if the sandbox UI doesn't eat the timeline.
- [strategy-player] The best idea in the whole set for someone who actually likes strategy: author rules, fight the entire hidden field daily, adapt to a real evolving metagame — Battlecode/Screeps done right, if it ever gets enough entrants to have a meta at all.

**Pitch.** Every day you get 20 minutes and a rulebook to design an autonomous commander, then every design in the world fights every other design head to head, and the top fifth of the field splits the pot.

**Core loop.** Daily cycle, one entry per wallet, $2 USDC. At 00:00 UTC a scenario seed is committed on-chain and immediately revealed — everyone sees the same 16x16 map, the same 12-item unit roster with the same point costs, the same objective. You have 24 hours to build a DOCTRINE: a 100-point army composition (typically 6-9 units) plus 12 behavior slots, each slot being (trigger, action, priority) picked from menus — 'if enemy within 4 tiles and my HP > 60%, advance on nearest objective, priority 7'. No code, drag-and-drop, but the rule space is roughly 10^14. The 30-second loop is the sandbox: change one slot, hit Run, watch a 300-tick battle resolve in the browser in under a second against three saved archetypes from yesterday's field, see the exact tick where your line broke, change it back. That loop is addictive on its own and it is free — you can sandbox all day without entering. At 00:00 UTC you must have a commit hash on-chain; a 30-minute reveal window follows; non-revealers forfeit their entry to the pool. Then every revealed doctrine plays every other doctrine (round-robin up to 150 entrants, 9-round Swiss above that), deterministic, no chance. Rank by wins, tie-break by objective margin. Top 20% of the field paid on a decreasing curve, 8% rake.

**Depth curve.** This is the concept where the day-100 answer is structural rather than hopeful. Your optimal doctrine is a best response to the FIELD, and the field is hidden until the deadline and changes every single day. That is a level-k reasoning problem with an evolving population — the same reason Axelrod's iterated prisoner's dilemma tournaments and Core War's hill stayed interesting for decades rather than months. Concretely: week one the field is 70% cheap-swarm doctrines, so anti-swarm area-control wins; week two everyone brings area control, so a fast two-unit objective rush beats it; week three counter-rush pickets appear and swarm comes back. There is no fixed solution to memorize, only a metagame to read. On top of that: (a) the full archive is free and replayable, so 'study yesterday's top 10 doctrines and run yours against the historical field' is a real training loop, like reviewing your chess games with an engine; (b) I ship exactly one new rule primitive or roster unit per week, which reliably detonates the current meta; (c) the scenario seed rotates the map and objective daily, so a doctrine tuned for chokepoints dies on an open map. A player on day 100 is not doing anything a day-1 player does — they are reading the field's composition distribution from public reveals and deliberately positioning off-meta. Strong players should place top-20% on 60-70% of days versus a novice's 20%, which compounds enormously over a month.

**Why on-chain.** Two things genuinely break as a normal web server, and one of them is the whole product. First, the commit: if I hold submissions on a server, nothing stops me (or anyone with DB access) from entering a late doctrine that best-responds to the field, and that single suspicion kills a competition where the prize depends on beating everyone else. On-chain commit-reveal with a hard slot deadline makes late best-response structurally impossible and publicly checkable. Second, verifiable settlement: the round-robin runs off-chain because 150 entrants is 11,175 battles at ~5ms each, but the result is anchored as a Merkle root on-chain alongside every revealed doctrine, and the engine is open source and bit-deterministic — so anyone can recompute the entire day and prove me wrong. Phase 2 makes that a real fraud proof: the same Rust engine compiled to BPF re-executes any single disputed battle on-chain inside the 1.4M CU transaction limit, and a successful challenge slashes my rake bond. That is the honest version of 'you can check the RNG' — here it is 'you can check the whole tournament'. Third, mundane but decisive: paying out 30 winners across 14 countries in USDC at settlement, with no payment processor that will touch a skill-prize business.

**Money flow.** $2 entry, 8% rake. 200 entrants = $400 in, $32 rake, $368 paid to the top 40 on a curve from ~$38 (1st) down to ~$3 (40th) — note that ranks 25-40 get back roughly their entry, which is deliberate: near-miss refunds are the cheapest retention mechanism in a daily contest. On-chain cost per entrant: 2 signatures (commit + reveal) at 5,000 lamports plus a claim = ~0.000015 SOL, about $0.0012, paid by the player. My compute cost is a $20/month VPS. At 200 entrants/day the rake is $960/month, which is not a business yet — the scaling levers are a $20 Masters division gated at top-100 ladder rating (40 entrants = $64/day rake) and, more importantly, a $9/month PRO subscription for the sandbox tools: unlimited historical field replays, composition distribution charts, batch-testing your doctrine against all 200 of yesterday's entries. ## STRAIN  —  47.0/80

*Lens: unused-primitives*

**Judge means** — depth 7.0 · skill 6.0 · tech 6.0 · chain 6.0 · econ 4.7 · cold start 5.7 · ship 5.3 · originality 6.3

- [advantage-player] Real optimization depth in the fitness landscape, but the designer's own risk section admits the epistasis could be shallow enough that the community maps the whole landscape and collapses to one genome by week six.
- [solo-shipper] Weekly VRF-revealed breeding puzzle with real epistatic depth; the author self-corrects to 5-6 weeks and hand-tunes the fitness function for days, which is the kind of honesty that should be rewarded.

**Pitch.** Every Monday a verifiable coin flip nobody can rig — not even me — publishes the week's environment, and you spend five days breeding a creature from the genes you actually own to survive it better than anyone else's.

**Core loop.** The week is the loop; inside it, decisions are 30-second beats.

MONDAY 00:00 UTC: Switchboard On-Demand VRF resolves the ENVIRONMENT — 16 public integer parameters (temperature, toxicity, forage density, predation pressure, pathogen virulence, seasonality, salinity, ...), each 0-255. Published immediately, in the open, before anyone breeds anything and before entry closes. Everyone sees the same problem at the same moment.

MON-FRI (the game): You own creatures. A creature is 48 genes, each a u8 — 48 bytes, stored as a compressed NFT. The fitness function is published Rust: 200 deterministic ticks of a small ecology sim, genome vs environment, output is an integer score. You can run it locally, instantly, unlimited times, for free. So the puzzle is fully transparent: you know the target, you know the scoring, you can evaluate any hypothetical genome in 3ms.

The constraint that makes it a game: you cannot type a genome. You can only BREED. Pick two creatures you own, choose a 48-bit crossover mask (gene i comes from parent A or B — this is your actual decision, 2^48 of them), and the child is fully deterministic: child gene i = mask ? A_i : B_i, then a deterministic mutation delta of -8..+8 derived from hash(parentA, parentB, mask, generation) applied to exactly 3 loci selected by the same hash. Nothing is rolled. You can compute the child before you commit. Hard constraint: sum of all 48 gene values <= 4,800, so every allele you gain costs one you give up.

That means the scarce resource is not compute — it is ALLELES YOU OWN. If nothing in your stock carries a high value at locus 12, no amount of breeding gets you there quickly; the deterministic mutation drifts +/-8 at three loci per generation, so reaching a needed allele from scratch takes many generations of directed breeding, or you buy a creature that already has it from another player. Breeding costs 0.002 SOL and is capped at 25 breeds per wallet per week.

FRIDAY 23:59: submit exactly ONE creature. Entry 0.05 SOL. Saturday: deterministic scoring, published traces. Sunday: purse pays out. Then Monday, a completely different environment, and half your stock is suddenly the wrong shape.

Session: 20-40 minutes on Monday (read the environment, plan), 10-15 minutes on three other days (execute breeds, watch the market), 5 minutes Friday (choose your entry).

**Depth curve.** This is the concept with the clearest honest answer to day 100, and it has three stacked layers.

(1) You learn the fitness landscape. Day 1 you hill-climb: change a gene, score goes up, keep it. Day 30 you have discovered that the function is heavily epistatic — locus 7 and locus 31 interact multiplicatively, locus 22 is a threshold gate that does nothing until it crosses 140 and then dominates. Serious players will be keeping notebooks. This is the same curve as competitive Pokemon breeding or Kaggle feature engineering, and it does not saturate for months because a 48-dimensional constrained landscape with designed interactions is genuinely large.

(2) You learn the ENVIRONMENT DISTRIBUTION. By week 20 you have seen 20 rolls and you can see the marginal distribution of each of the 16 parameters and their correlations. Now a strategic fork opens that did not exist on day 1: do you build SPECIALISTS (a stable of 8 lineages, each dominant in one environment archetype, most of them idle most weeks) or GENERALISTS (one robust lineage that finishes top-30 every week and never wins)? Specialists win purses; generalists compound stock. That decision is genuinely open and different players answer it differently — which is the definition of strategic depth.

(3) You accumulate a private allele library, and it is a real asset with real market value. This is the collection curve. By day 100 the top players own the only creatures carrying certain rare high-value alleles, which they mined over dozens of generations. New players cannot skip this with money alone because there are only so many carriers and the owners know what they have. This is the closest thing to a genuine sunk-effort moat in any of these three designs, and it is why the game gets MORE interesting for a veteran over time rather than less — your stock is a growing option set.

And critically: your stock persists across environments. A week you skip is not a week you lose, because the alleles keep. That is what makes this survive a missed month, which most competitive crypto games do not.

**Why on-chain.** A server could run every mechanic here. What a server cannot do is make the one claim the whole purse depends on: 'I did not know Monday's environment on Sunday.'

The operator generates the environment. The operator can also breed. If the environment is a server-side RNG call, the rational assumption — and it would be correct often enough — is that the operator or a friend pre-bred the winning genome to an environment they chose. There is no way to disprove this with a server. It is exactly the grievance that makes opaque gacha rates a live regulatory issue.

Switchboard On-Demand makes it disprovable: the randomness request transaction, the enclave-signed response, and the slot it was bound to are all on-chain, in order, permanently, and any player can replay the derivation from the response to the 16 published parameters using open-source code. The audit is not a promise, it is a script. 'Check the RNG yourself' is the product, not a disclaimer. Slot hashes would not do — they are validator-influenceable, and there is money on this.

The second thing that breaks on a server is the credible supply commitment. A creature's value comes from carrying alleles almost nobody else has. On a server I can mint a creature with any genome I want at any time, silently, and no player can ever verify I have not — which means allele scarcity is worth nothing and the market that makes layer (3) of the depth curve work never forms. On-chain, creatures are only creatable through the breed instruction, breeding requires owned parents, genesis stock is capped at 512 by an immutable program, and every creature's full ancestry is a public chain of hashes. Scarcity is checkable, so it is bankable.

Third, smaller: the purse settles globally in USDC/SOL in seconds to players in countries I could not open a payments relationship with.

**Money flow.** Three inflows, all consumption, no emissions, no token.

1. BREED FEE — 0.002 SOL per breed, capped at 25/wallet/week. This is the primary sink and it is paid for the thing players most want to do. 100% to the house. At 400 active players averaging 14 breeds/week: 11.2 SOL/week (~$900) to the house.
2. ENTRY FEE — 0.05 SOL per weekly entry, one per wallet. 90% to the purse, 10% house rake. At 400 entrants: purse 18 SOL (~$1,440), house 2 SOL.
3. MARKET FEE — 2% on every in-game creature sale, taken by the Token-2022 transfer fee extension at settlement.

Purse distribution across 400 entrants: 1st 15% (2.7 SOL, ~$216), 2nd 10%, 3rd 7%, 4th-10th 3% each, 11th-30th 1.35% each, 31st-100th 0.2% each. Paying 100 of 400 places matters: a game where 1 person of 400 gets paid trains 399 people to quit. A quarter of the field ending the week up something is what keeps the field.

House revenue: ~13 SOL/week (~$1,050) at 400 players, ~$4,000/week at 1,500 players. Modest, real, and it does not depend on the purse growing.

Genesis stock: 512 genesis creatures sold at 0.05 SOL each — AFTER the game is playable, in week 2, not before. That is 25.6 SOL (~$2,000) of bootstrap. It is deliberately tiny. Selling assets before shipping a game is how Ember Sword created a creditor class it could never repay; 512 creatures at $4 each creates no such class, and every one of them is immediately usable in a game that already runs.

The critical structural property: this economy has no faucet. Money leaves only via the purse, and the purse is exactly 90% of what entrants put in this week. It cannot decay when deposits slow, because the payout IS the deposits, minus rake, settled weekly with nothing carried forward. If 40 people play instead of 400, the purse is 1.8 SOL and the game is smaller but not broken. There is no yield, no staking, no promised APY, no emission schedule counting down. Creature market prices can fall to zero without breaking any obligation, because I never promised anyone that they would not.

**Legal structure (as generated).** Tier 1 (entry fee to purse, Skillz shape), but with a structural feature that makes it materially stronger than a standard Skillz-model game: at every point where a player pays or decides, ALL randomness has already been resolved and published.

The timeline is the argument. The VRF resolves Monday 00:00 and the environment is published immediately. Entry does not open until after publication. Every breeding decision, every crossover mask, every entry fee is paid with the full problem visible. Breeding is 100% deterministic — the crossover mask is chosen by the player, the mutation is a pure function of parent hashes, and the child is computable before you commit. There is no pull, no roll, no draw, no crit, no drop table, anywhere in the product.

So the contest is: everyone is handed the same fully-specified optimisation problem at the same instant, spends five days solving it with resources they own, and is ranked by a deterministic public scoring function. That is a chess problem with an entry fee. Under the predominance test it is not close. Under the MATERIAL ELEMENT test — the harsh one, ~30 states, where chance being material is enough even if skill predominates — the argument is still strong precisely because no chance element attaches to any player's outcome: the random draw selects the question, identically for everyone, before anyone answers, and does not touch the answers. This is the same posture as 'the crossword is randomly chosen but the contest is solving it'.

**Bots.** A bot will win. I am designing for that instead of pretending otherwise, and the design makes bots expensive rather than free.

Day one, a competent player writes a search that enumerates crossover masks over their owned stock and picks the highest-scoring child. This is trivially automatable — the fitness function is public and runs in 3ms. In most money games that ends the game. Here it does not, for one reason: the bottleneck is not search, it is GENETIC MATERIAL. The breed cap is 25/wallet/week. A bot with infinite compute and a weak allele library loses to a human with a great library, every time, because you cannot search your way to an allele you do not own. I deliberately constrained the resource bots have in unlimited supply and left the scarce resource ownership-bound.

Multi-accounting: 10 wallets buys 250 breeds/week — but each wallet needs its own stock, and stock cannot be concentrated without selling creatures between wallets (which costs the 2% market fee and, more importantly, splits the allele library across 10 shallow pools instead of one deep one). Deep libraries beat wide ones in a constrained-sum genome. The economics of sybil here are actively negative rather than merely policed.

Entry-fee farming: 10 wallets entering means 10 x 0.05 SOL of entry against a purse where paying places are ranked. Because 90% of entries return to the field, sybil entries are only profitable if your marginal alt genuinely places, and an alt with a shallow library does not.

What I accept honestly: the top 20 of the ladder will be automated, and a casual who breeds by hand will not crack it. That is true of every optimisation competition ever run (Kaggle, Robocode, Screeps, Battlecode), and those communities are healthy and long-lived. The mitigation is the payout curve — paying 100 of 400 places means a hand-player who understands the landscape lands in the top quarter and gets something. If I paid only the top 3, the game would be a bot arena with 397 unpaid extras and it would die in six weeks.

You would be consuming half a Solana block, network-wide, in a single game tick. The 12M CU per writable account limit is separately survivable (16 accumulators x 12M = 192M of headroom), so you correctly identified the account ceiling and missed the block ceiling. Many firms will simply not land in the target slot, and in a design where slot position IS the game, failing to land is losing.
  - 'CRANKLESS BY CONSTRUCTION' IS FALSE AT BOTH BOUNDARIES. Lazy settle-on-next-order works only for firms that submit a next order. A firm that skips tick 17 has undefined state that other firms' clearing computations may depend on; a firm that quits on tick 20 never settles at all; and the final tick of the season has no next order by definition, so season end requires a settlement pass over 100 firm accounts — which is a crank, needs a payer, and needs to be permissionless so it cannot be censored. Budget the 'anyone may settle any firm' instruction and the keeper that calls it, and stop claiming there is nothing to run.
  - TIME-PRIORITY RATIONING REQUIRES THE FULL ORDERED BOOK, NOT AGGREGATES. You describe 16 'Tick-Accumulator' PDAs but never size them. You cannot ration at the clearing price from aggregate supply — you need every order's slot, quantity and reserve price. 100 firms x ~200 B = ~20 KB, i.e. ~1.25 KB per shard, which is fine for account size and for the 64 MiB loaded-accounts limit, but it means every firm deserializes and scans 20 KB every tick and 100 firms redundantly recompute the identical clearing price 100 times. That is the 300-800k CU above. It works; it is just not the cheap design the doc implies.
  - UPGRADE AUTHORITY VOIDS THE NON-CUSTODY CLAIM (50 SOL of escrow per season is the largest pot of the three concepts, which makes this the most acute case). Freeze it or timelock through a multisig and say which.
  - RENT ARITHMETIC OMITS THE 128-BYTE HEADER, as in the other two. Season PDA at 2 KB is (2048+128) x 6,960 = 0.01514 SOL, Firm PDA at 512 B is (512+128) x 6,960 = 0.00445 SOL. Also unstated: who funds the 16 accumulator PDAs each tick, and whether they are reallocated per tick (realloc is capped at 10,240 bytes per instruction, which you will hit if a shard grows past that in one call).
- **Economic holes:**
  - THE PUBLISHED DEMAND CURVES MAKE THE GAME A REPEATED COURNOT OLIGOPOLY WITH PUBLIC ACTIONS, AND THAT HAS A CLOSED-FORM SOLUTION. Linear demand D(p) = a - bp with constants published in the docs, identical endowments, uniform clearing, n competitors: the static Nash quantity is textbook. Every quant who enters computes it in an afternoon — you concede as much ('those are solvable in an afternoon') and then claim the game is the other 99 firms. But with n=100 and public monitoring, the folk-theorem collusion you are hoping for is impossible (defection is undetectable in payoff terms and punishment is uncoordinable at that n), so the population converges toward Nash quantities and profit differences compress toward zero. When profits compress, rank is decided by the residual — which in your design is FILL PRIORITY, i.e. latency. The level-k layer you are selling collapses into 'play Nash, land in slot 0.' The tech tree and the fourth good add dimensionality but not a different equilibrium structure.
  - THE GUARANTEED POOL WITH DEV OVERLAY IS AN UNCAPPED SHORT OPTION, AND IT IS LARGEST AT LAUNCH. You promise 46 SOL of prizes across 25 places, guaranteed. At 20 firms you collect 10 SOL and owe 46 — YOU PERSONALLY PAY ~36 SOL (~$2,900) FOR ONE SEASON. At 40 firms you pay ~26 SOL (~$2,100). Worse, the payout table has 25 places: at any field below 25 entrants, every single entrant is guaranteed a prize, and the top prize alone (4 SOL) exceeds the entire entry fee of an 8-firm field. A sybil does not need to move the clearing price — your own anti-sybil arithmetic ('~10 coordinated firms costing 5 SOL to chase a 4 SOL prize, negative EV') is answering the wrong question. THE ATTACK IS NOT MANIPULATING THE PRICE, IT IS FILLING AN UNDER-SUBSCRIBED GUARANTEED POOL: 20 wallets pay 10 SOL and collect 46 SOL. +36 SOL per season, risk-free, no economic modelling required.
  - THE 32 BASELINE AGENTS ARE A FREE MONEY PUMP, AND YOU SAY SO YOURSELF WITHOUT PRICING IT. Open-source, published, deterministic and non-adaptive means their aggregate supply schedule is EXACTLY COMPUTABLE in advance. Any entrant simulates all 32 baselines, derives the residual demand curve precisely, and best-responds optimally with zero uncertainty. With 6 humans and 32 baselines, the game is not thin — it is a solved single-agent optimization against known opponents, and the humans' ranking is determined entirely by who wrote the simulator harness first. That is a worse game than a thin market, and it is also a subsidy: the baselines pay no entry but their presence lets a small number of entrants harvest a guaranteed 46 SOL pool.
  - REVENUE VS ADDRESSABLE POPULATION. 8% of 50 SOL = 4 SOL = $320 per 100-firm season. To reach $10k/month you need ~31 concurrent 100-firm divisions — 3,100 people paying $40/month to run spreadsheets against a synthetic commodity market. Prosperous Universe, the flagship of this exact niche, has spent seven years documenting on its own forums that the game is 'too slow,' that retention is the central problem, and that the persistent economy hollows out as players leave. That is the free version of your audience, and it is a few thousand people worldwide.
  - THE '5-15 MINUTES A DAY WITH A SPREADSHEET OPEN' LOOP HAS NO MOMENT OF TENSION. Thirty days to a payout, one transaction per day, no opponent you meet, no board, no round result — just a rank number that moves. Compare your own viral thesis (people post P&L screenshots): traders post P&L because the position was live and could have gone the other way in minutes. A daily-tick rank change at 30-day settlement produces the emotional profile of a mutual fund statement.
- **Cold start:** You identified this correctly and then chose the mitigation that makes it worse. At 6 firms the uniform clearing price is whatever the two most active people did, level-k reasoning degenerates into 'watch what Bob does' (your words), and the 25-place payout table pays every entrant several times over out of your pocket. The fix you propose — 32 deterministic, published, non-adaptive baseline agents — does not create depth, it creates a SOLVED environment: because the baselines are open-source and non-adaptive, their combined supply schedule is computable exactly, so the residual demand curve is known with certainty and the game becomes single-agent optimization against a fixed function. That is a math homework problem, not a market, and it is the strictly worse failure mode you already named ('farm the bots... a worse game and a slower death'). It is also worse than that in one respect you did not name: a solved environment means the top of the leaderboard is decided in week one and never changes, so the six humans who show up all lose to whoever wrote the harness, and they do not come back for season two. A 30-day season length means you get roughly twelve attempts per year to fix your cold start, which is the slowest iteration loop of the three designs by a factor of ten.
- **Prior art:** The closest on-chain analogue is 0xMonaco (Paradigm CTF 2022, later run as an invitational by Matchbox DAO): players submit immutable smart contracts as agents, resources are priced by a gradual Dutch auction, ELO decides the winner. It is Foundry's exact shape — a public, deterministic, on-chain economic game played by bots. What happened: it was tremendous fun for one weekend, it was immediately attacked rather than played (Zellic publicly documented building a highly profitable strategy by exploiting bugs in the game contract and getting disqualified for it), and it never became a product. Nobody runs 0xMonaco today. Note the failure mode carefully, because it is yours: in a fully on-chain deterministic economic game with money attached, the strongest 'strategy' is finding an arithmetic or rounding bug in the clearing function, and your clearing function is the most complex piece of on-chain code in any of these three designs.

Numerai is the closest live business: a paid/staked forecasting tournament, running since 2016, tens of millions paid out, participants stake and can be burned. It works. It required a token, a hedge fund, a decade, and a legal position ('staking is not an offer to participate in an investment contract, a security, a swap, or an interest in the fund') that a solo dev cannot replicate.

The game-design prior art is well-trodden and consistently niche. Prosperous Universe is a purely player-driven economic MMO whose own devlogs and community threads document exactly the problems this design will hit — 'the game is too slow' as the single biggest complaint, a public thread literally titled 'Player retention and the disappearing economy,' and the structural problem that departed players leave dead capital that new players must compete against. Offworld Trading Company proves the genre is genuinely fun — 78% positive across ~2,700 reviews — in REAL TIME, at small n, in a 45-minute match, which is the opposite of every cadence choice you made. Behind those sit the browser/play-by-mail econ sims (Utopia, Promisance, Earth 2025) whose daily-tick format peaked twenty years ago.

The bot-league prior art — Battlecode, Halite, Screeps, AICrowd, Kaggle — is uniformly FREE ENTRY WITH SPONSOR-FUNDED PRIZES. Not one of them charges. That is not an accident of taste; it is what keeps the field large enough to be interesting and keeps the whole gambling analysis off the table.
- **Suggested fix:** REPLACE SLOT-BASED TIME PRIORITY WITH A SEALED-BID FREQUENT BATCH AUCTION AND PRO-RATA RATIONING. Each tick becomes: a commit window (hash of your order sheet), then a reveal window, then a single uniform-price clearing in which oversubscription at the clearing price is rationed PRO-RATA, not by arrival time. This one change fixes more than anything else on the list. (1) It deletes the latency race outright — no reason to land in slot 0, so no Jito tip auction, no Helius-Sender edge, no 100-firms-in-one-slot congestion, and the 'local fee markets stay quiet' claim becomes true instead of false. (2) It restores the information trade-off you actually wanted: with sealed bids, submitting early versus late is a purely informational choice about how much of the public book you have seen, which is the tension the pitch describes and the current rule destroys. (3) It removes the redundant 100x recomputation of the clearing price, because clearing happens once per tick in a single permissionless settlement instruction over the 16 shards rather than being re-derived inside every firm's transaction — which also collapses your CU per firm-tick from 300-800k to something trivial. (4) It is the textbook answer (frequent batch auctions, Budish-Cramton-Shim) to exactly this pathology in real markets, so it is a design you can defend publicly and that your quant-adjacent target audience will immediately recognize and respect. (5) Commit-reveal introduces no randomness, so the zero-chance legal posture is untouched. Do this before writing any Anchor code, because it changes the account layout and eliminates the need to store the full ordered book. Then, in the same pass, replace the guaranteed 46 SOL pool with a published percentage table on actual entries plus a minimum-firm cancel-and-refund, and shorten the season from 30 daily ticks to 10 ticks over 5 days so you get 6 iterations a month instead of 1.

## BLIGHTWATER  —  45.7/80

*Lens: pve-and-coop*

**Judge means** — depth 7.0 · skill 6.3 · tech 5.3 · chain 7.7 · econ 4.7 · cold start 2.7 · ship 5.0 · originality 7.0

- [advantage-player] The cleanest case for real money as a costly, credible signal in the whole set -- bonded pacts among strangers is a genuinely chain-native mechanic -- but a single early defector can flip belief and race the whole commons to the bottom.
- [solo-shipper] The strongest true onChainNecessity claim in wave 2 (bonded, automatic slashing between strangers) -- but it's explicitly a 200-player design, and 8 players produces no tragedy and no commons.
- [strategy-player] Bonded cartels managing a shared, decaying resource is real political-economy depth, EVE-null-sec-adjacent, but it needs a defection cascade to not happen in week two, which is asking a lot of strangers.

**Pitch.** Two hundred players farm one shared watershed that dies if everyone takes as much as they want, and the only thing that makes a promise not to overharvest actually credible is that you locked fifty real dollars behind it.

**Core loop.** An eight-week Season on a shared map of 400 hexes; you run one colony with production buildings on hexes adjacent to your settlement. Ticks resolve every 8 hours. Each tick your colony converts labour into extraction across three resources (Silt, Reed, Ore) through a production chain with real logistics depth — Ore needs Reed for smelting, Reed regrows only on hexes below a blight threshold, and finished goods (Filters, Tools, Rations) are what score and what you trade. The antagonist is the BLIGHT INDEX, a single published function: B(t+1) = B(t) + k * max(0, E(t) - R), where E(t) is TOTAL extraction across all colonies this tick and R is the watershed's regeneration threshold. Above B=30 hexes go sterile permanently; above B=60 production yields halve; at B=100 the season ends early and every colony scores zero. Individually, extracting more is strictly better this tick. Collectively it is a countdown. There is no raiding, no combat, no elimination by another player — the only adversary is the arithmetic. THE MECHANIC THAT MAKES IT A GAME: a PACT is an on-chain account where N colonies each escrow a USDC bond (typically $10-50) and specify a per-tick extraction cap. The program already holds every colony's extraction data, so compliance is checked automatically at each tick with no oracle and no arbiter. Exceed your cap and your bond is slashed and distributed pro-rata to the compliant members. That converts cheap talk into a costly signal, which is the entire difference between a Discord promise and an enforceable treaty. Second layer: PUBLIC WORKS — a filtration works on a hex reduces B for everyone, a pure free-rider problem, funded through dominant-assurance contracts (pledge USDC toward a threshold; if the threshold is missed everyone is refunded plus a 10% bonus funded from charter revenue, making early pledging dominant over waiting). Session: 4-8 minutes of colony management per tick, twice a day, plus unbounded negotiation.

**Depth curve.** Day 1 you are learning that Reed regrows and Ore does not. Day 100 — season two, week four — you are running a five-colony cartel with a tiered bond schedule, arguing whether to admit a colony whose on-chain compliance history shows two slashes in season one, and working out whether the eleven unaffiliated colonies in the eastern basin will free-ride hard enough to make the filtration pledge fail. The depth is political and player-generated, so it does not run out and needs no content updates: the strategy space is coalition formation, bond sizing (too small and it does not bind, too large and nobody joins), enforcement design, cartel-busting, and reputation. On-chain compliance history is permanent and public, so a season-three player carries a two-season record that materially changes who will pact with them — the single most valuable thing a persistent ledger contributes to a social game, and it compounds rather than decays. Underneath the politics sits a genuine production-optimization game with a satisfying solved-locally / unsolvable-globally structure. The specific claim about day 100: the sim is identical, but the counterparties know you, and that is a different game. Honest caveat: this depth requires a live social scene — a season with 12 disengaged players is a spreadsheet.

**Why on-chain.** This is the concept where removing the chain removes the game. The core mechanic is a costly, automatically-enforced commitment between mutually distrustful strangers, needing three things simultaneously that no game server provides: (1) real money at stake — an in-game-points bond is not a costly signal because points are free to the person promising, and the design collapses back to Discord promises, which twenty years of Diplomacy prove are worth nothing; (2) enforcement neither party and no operator can veto, reverse, or be lobbied over — the program slashes the bond because the extraction number in the account exceeded the cap, with no arbiter, no support ticket, and no me; (3) parameters that provably cannot be tuned — the blight function is the game's entire trust surface, and a commons game where players suspect the operator quietly softened the curve to keep whales happy is dead, so a published program with upgrade authority burned at season start is not decoration, it is the product. Add the map: generated from a Switchboard On-Demand VRF draw published with its proof at season open, so nobody including me chose which colony sits next to the good Ore. And instant global sub-cent settlement means a player in Lagos and a player in Lisbon bond a treaty in ninety seconds with no bank — genuinely on the honest short list of what crypto adds. Notably this game does NOT need MagicBlock: ticks are eight hours apart, plain L1 at 400ms slots is wildly overspecified, and adding a rollup would be fashion rather than need.

**Money flow.** IN, one primary stream and one secondary. (1) CHARTERS: 0.05 SOL (~$4) buys one colony for one season. Colonies genuinely die — bankruptcy, sterile hexes, blight collapse — and a dead colony's owner buys another to re-enter, so expect ~1.8 charters per player per season. Seasons are eight weeks, which is the answer to 'why buy again next month': the season ends, the map is re-drawn by VRF, the blight parameters rotate (R, k and the sterility thresholds are published pre-season and differ), and everyone starts over with only reputation carried forward. (2) STEWARDSHIP MARKET: you can delegate operation of your colony to another player for a negotiated USDC fee while you travel; the program enforces the delegation's scope (a steward can run production but cannot withdraw stockpiles or sign pacts) and the developer takes 3%. That is where players EARN — by selling labour and expertise to other players who value it, which is a purchase, not predation. WHAT THE DEVELOPER DOES NOT TOUCH: bonds. Zero rake on pact bonds, ever. Taking a percentage of slashed bonds would convert a performance-bond mechanic into a raked wager and is the single fastest way to turn this into something a regulator recognizes. Bonds are non-custodial, developer-inaccessible, and route only between pact members. ARITHMETIC: 1,000 players per season x 1.8 charters x $4 = $7,200 per eight-week season, ~$3,600/month. Stewardship at 120 arrangements per season averaging $25 = $3,000 volume, $90 to the developer. Public-works assurance bonuses cost ~$300/season out of charter revenue. Developer nets roughly $3,400/month. SINKS: the charter is consumed by the season ending — the cleanest possible consumption sink, because the thing you bought is explicitly temporary and everyone knows it going in. There is NO pool paying returns to depositors: the developer pays players nothing, players earn only from other players buying their labour, and bonds are the players' own money returned to them for keeping their word. A ponzi is not constructible from these parts.

**Legal structure (as generated).** Tier 0 with one deliberate mechanic that must be built precisely. CHANCE IS REMOVED COMPLETELY: the simulation is fully deterministic, weather and events are published as a fixed schedule at season open (information to plan around, not variance), and the only randomness is the VRF map draw which happens before anyone plays and is identical for everyone — symmetric, pre-play, therefore not a chance element in any player's outcome. With zero chance the material-element test has nothing to bite on and predominance is trivially satisfied. THE BOND IS THE THING TO GET RIGHT. A performance bond is not a wager: the outcome depends entirely on the bonded party's own voluntary compliance with a term they agreed to, there is no chance, and there is no house. It is structurally a security deposit or liquidated-damages clause; the closest real-world analogues are bail bonds and escrowed construction performance bonds, both plainly not gambling. But a determined pair of users could WRAP a bet inside it — two people pact with no intention to comply and use the slash as a payout. Structural hardening, all enforced in code: bonds capped at 5x the charter price (max $20 per pact per colony); a colony needs 7 days of recorded activity before it can post a bond; slashes distribute PRO-RATA TO ALL COMPLIANT MEMBERS BY DEGREE OF COMPLIANCE, never to a single counterparty and never winner-take-all, which destroys the two-party payout geometry a bet requires; pacts require a minimum of three colonies; no colony may be in more than four pacts. Together these make the bond useless as a betting instrument while leaving it fully functional as a commitment device. MONEY TRANSMISSION: the program is non-custodial throughout — bonds sit in PDAs the developer cannot touch, stewardship fees route atomically, and the developer's only receipts are charter sales (sale of a game licence) and a 3% marketplace fee (a services marketplace), keeping this in the FinCEN 2019 non-custodial-software lane. UK: no chance, no wager, no prize won by chance, and the charter is a consumable licence rather than money's worth won. Nothing touches sweepstakes, dual currency, offshore licensing or prediction contracts. Standard terms of service, no state geofencing required, and a written-in-advance memo explaining the bond structure ready if anyone asks.

**Bots.** Bots will play this well and that is thematically correct: a colony optimizing extraction under a cap is a linear-programming problem, and the defector taking the individually rational amount is literally the antagonist the game is about. So bots are permitted and expected; the interesting part is that a bot cannot negotiate a pact, cannot build a reputation other colonies trust, and cannot participate in an assurance contract requiring judgment about whether a threshold will be met — the parts of the game generating the most value are the hardest to automate. A bot colony refusing all pacts gets out-produced over eight weeks by a human cartel keeping blight low, because the blight function punishes unrestrained extraction at the aggregate level and there is no way to opt out of a shared watershed. MULTI-ACCOUNTING is the real vector: someone buys 10 charters at $4 each and dominates a basin. Three structural checks. (1) Charters are cheap but the map is not — hexes are exclusive and adjacent, so a sybil's colonies compete with each other for the same hexes and cannibalize their own production chain. (2) More colonies means more extraction means faster blight, so a sybil scaling up accelerates the thing that kills their own position. (3) Pact admission is a human decision and on-chain compliance history is public, so a cluster of new colonies appearing the same day from the same funding wallet is visible to everyone and simply does not get admitted to treaties, which is where the real returns are. I would rather lean on those than on KYC or proof-of-personhood, which a solo dev cannot operate.

**First session.** 0:00 — someone links the PUBLIC TREATY LEDGER, the shareable artifact: a page listing every pact in the current season, its members, its caps, and its compliance record, with three colonies flagged SLASHED and a comment thread arguing about it. Drama is the ad. 1:00 — you click SEASON 3 OPENS IN 6 DAYS and see the map preview with the VRF proof and the published blight parameters, plus a full-resolution replay of season 2's collapse rendered as an animated heat map going green to brown over eight weeks. 3:00 — the tutorial is a solo sandbox against a simulated 20-colony watershed, no wallet, ~10 minutes, ending by showing you that your optimal individual play killed the basin in week five. That is the pitch, delivered as an experience. 13:00 — CLAIM A CHARTER: 0.05 SOL, and the wallet appears here for the first time. You have no SOL. The page offers Coinbase Onramp / MoonPay at a $5 minimum and, more practically for a first-timer, a checkout taking a card and delivering the charter to a wallet created in-browser via an embedded wallet, with a plain 'export your key' path when you are ready. Funding is $4 plus about $0.15 of buffer SOL for a season of fees, which the charter purchase provides automatically. 15:00 — you pick a starting hex from the unclaimed set, place a Reed farm and a Silt pit, and set your first tick's labour split. One transaction, ~5,000 lamports. 16:00 — the site pushes you toward the season Discord and specifically at the PACT FINDER, listing open pacts accepting members with their caps and bond sizes. You are 45 minutes from your first tick resolve and you have a decision to make about whether $10 is worth a promise.

**Viral mechanic.** The public treaty ledger and the slash notifications. Every slash is a small, legible, permanent story — 'GREYMARSH broke the Eastern Basin pact at tick 47, bond of $30 distributed to seven compliant colonies' — with names, numbers and a receipt, exactly the shape of thing that spreads because it is gossip with proof attached. Season-end produces two artifacts: an animated heat map of the watershed's eight-week decline (or, rarely, its survival), and a per-colony reputation card showing pacts held and pacts broken, which people post because it is a personal record and because 'never slashed across three seasons' is a genuine claim about your character that anyone can verify. Recruiting follows the mechanic: pacts want more members because a pact that does not cover a basin does not control the blight, so existing players have an in-game reason to bring in people they can vouch for. Cold start is solved on the same principle — season zero runs on a 60-hex map with 6-20 colonies and I run it myself with eight friends; the blight function is parameterized by colony count so the arithmetic works identically at 6 as at 200.

**Four-week MVP.** SHIP: 60 hexes, up to 20 colonies, 3 resources with a 2-step production chain, 8-hour ticks, the blight function with published parameters, colony PDAs, the two-level aggregation crank, and PACTS with a single fixed template (flat per-tick extraction cap plus a USDC bond, three-member minimum, pro-rata slash). CSS hex grid coloured by blight level, unicode glyphs for buildings, no art. Discord is the entire negotiation layer. Week 1: write the economy simulator OUTSIDE the chain and run 500 synthetic seasons to tune k, R and the sterility thresholds until the honest answer to 'is restraint actually better than defection over eight weeks' is yes but only above ~60% participation — if that tuning fails, the game does not exist and I want to know in week one, not week four. Week 2: Anchor program — colonies, ticks, extraction, aggregation crank, blight. Week 3: pacts, USDC escrow, automatic compliance evaluation, slashing. Week 4: web client, hex map, tutorial sandbox, treaty ledger page, deploy, run season zero with eight people on a 20-hex map. EXPLICITLY CUT: public works and dominant-assurance contracts (v2 — the best mechanic in the design and still cut, because pacts must be proven first), the stewardship market (v2), VRF map generation (v1 uses a hash-committed seed published before season open, delivering the same fairness property for zero integration cost), variable pact templates, the third production tier, and any in-app chat. If week 3 slips, ship without bonds and run season zero as an unenforced-promises control group — a genuinely useful experiment, because it measures exactly how much the bond is worth.

**Biggest risk.** A defection cascade in week two, killing the season before the political metagame forms. Commons games have a brittle equilibrium: restraint is only rational if you believe most others will restrain, so a visible early defector can flip belief and the basin races to the bottom by week three, leaving six dead weeks of spreadsheet. Three mitigations, none complete: front-load the sterility thresholds so the first hexes to go sterile are the over-extractors' own adjacent hexes, making early defection self-punishing; ship pact templates and a pact finder so coordination has scaffolding instead of requiring someone to invent it in Discord; keep seasons at eight weeks so a failed season is a bounded loss rather than a dead product. Second risk is the one this repo's wave-1 review already identified about social-commitment games: abandonment. An idle colony must degrade only itself — no pact can be broken by absence alone (the cap is an upper bound, so doing nothing complies) and an inactive colony's extraction goes to zero, which helps the commons rather than hurting it. That is a deliberate inversion of the webDiplomacy failure mode where the absentee wrecks their allies. Third risk is the honest one: this game is only as good as its social scene, I do not own that scene, it lives on Discord, and if forty people do not show up caring about the same watershed there is nothing here but a farming spreadsheet with a rent-exempt account attached.

**Solana primitives.**
  - One colony PDA per player (~600 bytes) holding stockpiles, buildings and cumulative extraction — sharded per player, so all player writes land in independent local fee markets with near-zero priority fees and no contention against the 12M CU per writable account per block ceiling
  - Two-level crank aggregation for the global Blight Index: colonies write extraction to their own PDA; a permissionless crank folds them into 16 REGION accumulator PDAs, which fold into one BLIGHT PDA — the single global hot account is therefore written 3 times per day rather than once per player action, which is the whole reason this design does not hit the writable-account ceiling
  - PACT accounts as USDC (SPL token) escrow PDAs with the cap schedule inline; compliance is evaluated against on-chain extraction data at tick close, so slashing needs no oracle, no arbiter and no off-chain signature
  - Dominant-assurance PUBLIC WORKS contracts as threshold escrow PDAs with a refund-plus-bonus path funded from charter revenue, resolved by clock
  - Switchboard On-Demand VRF drawn once at season open for map generation, proof published on-chain before charters activate — verifiable randomness as a consumer fairness feature, symmetric and pre-play, introducing no per-player chance
  - Program upgrade authority burned at season start so blight parameters are provably immutable for the season; next season is a new deployment with published diffs
  - Rent economics used deliberately: a 600-byte colony PDA costs ~0.0042 SOL rent-exempt at ~6,960 lamports/byte and is CLOSED and refunded at season end, so a season leaves no dust
  - Player transaction cost: ~4 signed transactions per day at 5,000 lamports = 0.0006 SOL/month (~$0.05); an entire season's fee burden per player is under a dime
  - Jito bundles for multi-step atomic actions (join pact + post bond + set cap must not partially apply) within the 5-transaction bundle limit

### Hostile review

*backfilled 2026-08-21; reviews the raw concept, not the later build spec*

- **Verdict:** buildable-with-caveats
- **Solo-dev estimate:** 5 weeks
- **Degenerate strategy:** The blight function B(t+1)=B(t)+k*max(0, E(t)-R) is fully public with published parameters, so the maximum sustainable aggregate extraction rate (E(t) <= R keeps B flat forever) is a one-time arithmetic problem, not an ongoing decision. A cartel that solves it once at season start and locks pact caps accordingly has nothing left to decide for the remaining seven weeks beyond confirming it stayed under an already-fixed number — the stated '4-8 minutes twice a day' becomes a rote check-the-box loop for anyone inside a stable pact, and the production-optimization mechanics (the bulk of the actual simulation) only matter during the brief unsolved window before caps stabilize, or for colonies that never join a pact. The production game and the politics game crowd each other out: capped colonies don't need to optimize production, and uncapped colonies are racing to extract before collapse, so precision optimization doesn't matter there either.
- **Technical blockers:**
  - The 'automatic, no-oracle, no-arbiter' compliance claim is true for where the data lives but understates the machinery needed to act on it: someone must run a permissionless crank, per active pact, per tick close, comparing each member's this-tick extraction delta against their cap and triggering a slash on violation. At a realistic 200-colony season (4-pact cap per colony, 3-colony pact minimum), that's plausibly 150-250 independent compliance-check transactions every 8 hours for the whole season — a real, recurring workload absent from the given CU/fee budget, which prices only player-side transactions. Likely self-resolving economically (a compliant pact member has a direct incentive to trigger the check and claim a pro-rata share of a violator's bond, the same self-interested-liquidator pattern DeFi relies on), but it is neither designed nor budgeted in the current writeup, and per-tick extraction needs an explicit delta field in the 600-byte colony PDA that isn't itemized either.
  - Minor arithmetic slip: applying the doc's own rent formula ((bytes+128) x 6,960 lamports, verified against its own 165-byte and 10 MiB examples) to a 600-byte colony PDA gives (600+128) x 6,960 = 5,067,680 lamports = 0.00507 SOL, not the stated '~0.0042 SOL' — about a 20% undercount. Immaterial in dollar terms (~$0.05) but worth catching before the 'under a dime per season' fee claim is repeated as a hard number.
- **Economic holes:**
  - The legal hardening that makes the bond safe (capped at 5x charter price, ~$20 max; pro-rata distribution only, never winner-take-all; 3-colony minimum) directly undercuts the bond's job as a costly commitment device. A colony that complied all season specifically to build trust can rationally defect hard in a pact's final tick(s), where the shadow of the future collapses within that pact instance: the maximum loss is the already-known $20 bond, and the reputation cost attaches to a colony identity that costs only $4 to replace via a fresh charter next season. A one-shot 'burn a $4 identity for a late-season extraction spike bigger than $20' strategy is not priced out.
  - 'Finished goods... are what you trade' implies a real player-to-player market, but no sink, source, fee, or mechanism for that trade appears in moneyFlow, which lists only Charters and the 3% Stewardship fee as developer revenue. Either finished-goods trading is a real, valuable, entirely unaccounted-for subsystem, or the coreLoop's 'what you trade' claim overstates what the MVP actually delivers.
- **Cold start:** The strongest cold-start design of the three, deliberately so: the blight function is parameterized by colony count, so a 6-20 colony 'season zero' produces mechanically the same game as a 200-colony season, and the design frames a small run as a genuinely useful experiment rather than a degraded version of the real thing. At 8 players the production sim, the blight arithmetic, and a minimal 3-colony pact all function. What's still missing is the same acquisition flywheel every concept here shares: the treaty-ledger drama and slash-notification virality that IS the viral mechanic needs a large enough population producing legible betrayals and public reputations to be spectator content — an 8-person watershed's public treaty ledger is a private group chat with extra steps.
- **Bot extraction:** Because ticks are only 3/day and there's no penalty for declining to join any pact (only for exceeding a cap you voluntarily accepted), a swarm of cheap ($4) charters that never negotiates and simply submits the maximum-extraction transaction the instant every tick window opens can front-load a disproportionate share of the season's early 'cheap' yield — before blight accumulates, before yields halve at B=60, and before humans have had time to organize any pact (the design's own week-2 defection-cascade risk implies pact formation takes real days). A human checking in twice daily loses early-window extraction share to an always-on script with zero missed ticks. Partially self-limiting (unrestrained aggregate extraction drives the shared blight toward the B=100 collapse that zeroes every colony's score, bot included), but the bot has already banked its disproportionate early extraction before that collapse is visible, and the tradeable value of that banked extraction is itself underspecified (see economicHoles).
- **Prior art:** Searched specifically and found no shipped crypto game combining a production-based tragedy-of-the-commons simulation with on-chain bonded/slashed treaties; academic 'commons game' work (Ostrom-style multi-population resource-game papers) exists only as research simulation. The closest actual shipped analogue, on the identical Solana/MagicBlock stack this concept would use, is MagicBlock's own reference game Colony (a shared-resource city-builder on Solana Mobile Seeker) — but it is a lighter, non-monetary, non-bonded design with two factions competing for shared resources, and no public player-count, retention, or revenue data for it could be found, itself informative about how far even a simpler version of this genre has gotten with real backing. Dominant-assurance contracts (Tabarrok) have working smart-contract reference implementations dating to 2018, but no evidence was found of one used inside a live game rather than as a funding-mechanism demo.
- **Suggested fix:** Move the deterrent from the bond (capped low for good legal reasons that shouldn't be relitigated) to the reputation ledger, and make the reputation flag follow the funding wallet's charter-purchase history rather than the disposable colony/PDA identity alone. A colony that defects in a pact gets a permanent, non-decaying public flag tied to the wallet that funded its charter, not just to that season's colony account — closing the '$4 buys a clean identity next season' laundering path without touching the bond cap, the pro-rata distribution rule, or any other piece of the existing legal hardening.

## REDOUBT  —  44.0/80

*Lens: wagered-strategy-a*

**Judge means** — depth 6.0 · skill 5.3 · tech 5.7 · chain 4.7 · econ 4.7 · cold start 6.3 · ship 5.7 · originality 5.7

- [advantage-player] Defense retains real opponent-modeling skill, but the assault half is a fully-specified public optimization problem that a weekend solver cracks -- half the game gets professionalized fast.
- [solo-shipper] Fully async two-phase fortress duel that correctly refuses a rollup it doesn't need -- the most infra-conservative design in wave 2, though the assault phase degrades to solved-bot territory fast.
- [strategy-player] Clever two-phase build-then-attack structure that teaches two different skills, but the attack half is a textbook search problem a weekend solver eats alive.

**Pitch.** You and one opponent each build a fortress on the identical map, then each of you gets one shot at cracking the other's — whoever solves the enemy puzzle better takes the pot, and neither of you is ever waiting for the other to be online.

**Core loop.** A match is four sealed submissions across two phases on one committed 20x20 map. PHASE 1 (both blind, simultaneous): each player spends a 1,000-point budget placing up to 40 pieces from an 8-item roster — walls (5pts), slow fields (40), splash emplacements (120), single-target lances (90), rerouters (60) — to maximise the distance and damage an attacker must survive between the two fixed spawn/exit tiles. Commit hash, then reveal; both defenses become public. PHASE 2 (both blind, simultaneous): each player now spends 1,000 points on an assault against the OPPOSING defense — a wave list of up to 30 entries, each entry being (unit type, spawn tick, lane) drawn from 6 attacker types with different speed/armor/HP profiles. Commit, reveal. Both assaults resolve deterministically over up to 600 ticks. Your score is total HP delivered to the exit, tie-broken by ticks elapsed. Higher score wins. The 30-second loop in phase 2 is: 'his splash emplacement covers the third bend — do I send 12 cheap fast units to bleed its cooldown, or 3 armored units that survive it but arrive too slowly to beat the lance stack?' You test it, watch the replay, adjust one spawn tick, test again. Blitz mode: 10 minutes per phase, ~25 minute match. Standard: 12 hours per phase, so the match runs 2 days and nobody has to be online at the same time. Stakes $5 / $20, 8% rake.

**Depth curve.** The two halves teach different and non-substitutable skills, which is why day 100 differs from day 1. Attacking is an optimization problem against a KNOWN target — pure solving, and you get measurably better at it (a strong attacker delivers 3-4x the HP a novice does against the same defense). Defending is a MINIMAX problem against an UNKNOWN adversary: you are not building the defense that stops the best attack, you are building the defense that maximises the expected shortfall of the attack distribution the field actually produces. Those are different mental muscles and the second one never gets solved because the attacking population keeps improving. Concretely: day 1 you build a long maze because long looks good. Day 10 you learn that mazes lose to fast-cheap floods and that damage density beats path length. Day 30 you learn to build asymmetric defenses with a deliberate visible weakness that funnels attackers into a kill pocket — a trap that only works while the field has not seen it. Day 100 you have an archetype library, you recognise an opponent's defense family in eight seconds, and you have a rehearsed counter-comp for each. The archive is the training ground: every defense ever submitted is public and replayable for free, so 'grind 50 historical defenses' is a real practice regimen with an objective score. A strong player should clear 80% against a novice comfortably, because both phases compound.

**Why on-chain.** Being blunt: the ephemeral rollup buys this game nothing — four submissions per player over hours, each under 200 bytes, is an L1 workload and I would be adding a dependency for a badge. What the chain does buy is the entire integrity model. The two phases only work if simultaneity is real: if I hold defenses on a server, the second player to submit could see the first's and win every match, and no amount of promising otherwise fixes it. On-chain commit-reveal with a slot-enforced deadline makes 'both blind' a fact rather than a policy. Same for the assault phase. Second: escrow and atomic payout between strangers with no accounts, across borders, settling in seconds, with a program PDA that I cannot drain. Third: the defense archive is only trustworthy as a training corpus if the defenses were genuinely committed before the assaults that faced them — the chain timestamps that for free. Match resolution itself runs off-chain (a 600-tick 20x20 sim blows past 1.4M CU), anchored by a result hash with an interactive bisection fraud proof as phase 2: challenger and operator binary-search the disputed tick range, and the single disputed tick is executed on-chain in under 200k CU.

**Money flow.** $5 duel: $10 in, 8% rake = $0.80 treasury, $9.20 winner. On-chain cost: 4 signatures per player = 8 x 5,000 lamports = 0.00004 SOL (~$0.0032), paid by players, plus refundable rent. That is a 0.4% cost against the rake, the cheapest of the three concepts precisely because it refuses the rollup. The async score-duel format (both players attack the SAME archived third-party defense, blind, higher score wins) is $2 with the same 8% rake and only 2 signatures per player. 300 duels/day at mixed stakes ~= $180/day rake. Value enters as USDC from players; exits as winnings; no token, no emission, no asset sale, no NFT, nothing where old participants need new ones. Say the ugly part: minus rake this is zero-sum and the median player is down 8% per match. The non-extractive line is a $7/month DRILL subscription — unlimited attempts against the full historical defense archive with a scored leaderboard, batch-solver comparison, and heatmaps of where your assaults die. That is selling practice, and practice demand in a skill game is real and recurring. 20% of rake escrows a weekly $ open bracket to give the ladder a peak.

**Legal structure (as generated).** Tier 1, and structurally the tightest of the three because both players perform IDENTICAL tasks on an IDENTICAL committed map with symmetric information at every step. Consideration and prize survive; CHANCE IS ABSENT: the map seed is committed and revealed before anyone acts, both players receive the same map and the same 1,000-point budgets, defenses are revealed simultaneously so neither assault is informed by more than the other, and the tower-defense resolution is integer-deterministic with no crits, no dice, no drops, no variance of any kind. Both players attack and both defend, so there is not even a first-mover role asymmetry to argue about. Passes predominance, passes MATERIAL ELEMENT (no chance element exists to be material), passes any-chance. The async score-duel is deliberately structured the same way — two strangers each attack the SAME archived defense, blind, which is symmetric — and NOT as a 'post your fortress with a bounty and let challengers pay to try it' mode, which I considered and am cutting: a standing challenge where one side risks nothing and repeatedly farms weaker challengers functions like a house bank, and regulators look at what a product functionally IS, not what it is called. Required regardless: state geofence excluding Florida (statutory bar on wagering on skill games) and any state counsel flags, 18+ verification, deposit and match-frequency limits, self-exclusion, per-state written opinion before opening. Not built: no sweepstakes/dual-currency (Tier 2 statutes name suppliers, promoters and platform providers personally, with felony exposure in NY/OK/LA), no offshore license, no token, no prediction framing. Defenses and assaults are public copyable data, never NFTs, so there is no secondary market and therefore no UK 'money's worth' problem and nothing to trip Google Play's earnings-promotion ban.

**Bots.** This is the concept most exposed to bots and I will not soften it: assault optimization against a fully known defense is a textbook search problem, and a competent local-search solver with a few seconds of compute will beat almost every human at phase 2. That is the single biggest design threat. Three responses. First, the clock: blitz mode gives 10 minutes per phase, and I gate all stakes above $5 to blitz, so the solver has to run inside a window and the human is not fighting an overnight compute budget. Second, the defense phase is genuinely bot-resistant in a way phase 2 is not — building a defense means maximising expected shortfall against the distribution of attacks the field will actually produce, which requires a model of the population, not a search over a known target. Since defense and assault are weighted equally in the final score, a pure solver bot with a naive defense loses to a strong human roughly as often as it wins. Third, economics: at $5 stakes with an 8% rake the maximum solver edge is roughly $0.40 per 25-minute blitz match, under $1/hour per instance, and I cap concurrent matches at 2 per wallet with 72-hour wallet aging and a prior deposit. Detection is timing fingerprints and assault-plan entropy signatures. Honest bottom line: if this game gets big, solvers own the assault phase and the meta becomes 'everyone attacks near-optimally, defense design decides everything' — which is still a real game, but it is a different game than I designed, and I should plan for it rather than be surprised by it.

**First session.** Minute 0: they arrive on a shared replay — a 30-second CSS/emoji animation of an assault dissolving against a fortress with a leak counter ticking. Minute 0-4: below it, 'You try': the same defense loaded in a free sandbox, 1,000 points of attackers, unlimited attempts, no wallet, no signup. Their first attempt leaks 40 HP; the replay scrubber shows exactly where the wave died; their fourth attempt leaks 900. That escalation from 40 to 900 in four minutes IS the hook and it is entirely free. Minute 4-6: 'Beat someone's score for $2' — the async score-duel, which is the on-ramp because it needs no live opponent. Wallet connect (Phantom/Backpack) or an in-browser passkey wallet with no seed phrase; state selector and 18+ gate, IP-checked; blocked states keep the full free sandbox, the full archive, and an unranked ladder permanently. Minute 6-10: funding — USDC address/QR plus a card on-ramp at a $10 minimum, and a faucet PDA drips 0.01 SOL for signatures. Minute 10-13: they commit an assault on today's archive defense (one signature), reveal (one signature), and immediately see their score against the benchmark. Minute 13-20: matched against the next player who took the same defense — result lands as a notification, USDC settles into their wallet, and the losing replay is right there to study. Their first full 2-phase duel comes on day 2, from the ladder.

**Viral mechanic.** Every match produces two artifacts and both are shareable and both are playable. The first is 'crack my fortress': a link to your defense in a free sandbox with a leak-score target and a leaderboard of everyone who has attempted it. That is a puzzle you WANT to send to a friend, it costs nothing to attempt, and it is the single best free-to-paid funnel here because attempting a fortress is the exact skill the paid mode tests. The second is the assault replay — a 90-byte deterministic wave list encoded in the URL, so 'watch me solve this in 41 ticks' plays back in the browser with no server and no video. There is a natural leaderboard scene: the fortress that has survived the most attempts, and the player with the best average leak across the archive, both public and both worth chasing. Channels are web-first plus the Solana Mobile dApp Store (200k+ Seekers, no crypto restrictions, no 30% cut); the audience I actually target is tower-defense and optimization players (Bloons TD Battles, Mindustry, Zachtronics, Screeps communities), where the pitch is 'PvP tower defense with real minimax depth' and crypto is a payment rail I barely mention — which is the only honest way around the acquisition contradiction, since the channels that reach real gamers forbid earnings talk.

**Four-week MVP.** Week 1: deterministic simulation core in no_std Rust — 20x20 grid, pathfinding with rerouters, 8 defensive pieces, 6 attacker types, 600-tick resolution, fixed-point integers, zero floats. Compiled to wasm (browser) and native (server), fuzzed for cross-target bit-identity. This is the hardest week and pathfinding determinism is where the bugs live. Week 2: the two editors — defense placement (drag pieces onto a CSS grid, live 'best-known-attack' preview score) and assault composer (wave list with spawn ticks and lanes) — plus the tick scrubber replay. Emoji and CSS only. Week 3: Anchor program — match PDA, USDC vault, two-phase commit/reveal with Clock deadlines, claim_forfeit, resolve, payout, close_vault, treasury rake; plus the async score-duel path against archive defenses. Week 4: geofence, 18+ gate, RG tooling and limits, the free sandbox/archive site (which is also the entire marketing site), landing layer with Helius Sender and correct CU budgeting, durable-nonce pre-signed reveals for 12-hour standard mode. Explicitly cut: the bisection fraud proof (v1 publishes the engine commit hash and every reveal so anyone can recompute; the trust assumption is stated on the front page, not hidden), MagicBlock ER, ELO ladder and seasons, the DRILL subscription, the weekly bracket, mobile app, and every roster item beyond the launch 14.

**Biggest risk.** Solvers eating the assault phase. Unlike SALVO, where a bot has to model a human, and unlike DOCTRINE, where the target is hidden, phase 2 here is a fully-specified optimization problem with a public objective — someone will write a decent local search in a weekend and post it, and the day that tool is public the assault half of the game is settled and the wagering becomes 'who has the better solver', which is a legitimate but much smaller game and a legally messier one. The mitigations (blitz clocks, equal weighting with the bot-resistant defense phase) reduce it and do not remove it. Second risk: engine determinism. Everything here — the commit-reveal integrity, the archive, the future fraud proof, the fairness claim — rests on the pathfinder producing bit-identical output on every target, and a single float, hash-iteration-order, or tie-break inconsistency between wasm and native produces disputed matches with real money attached and no way to prove who was right. That is a week of the four spent on property tests and it will still be the thing that breaks. Third, as always: liquidity, though the async score-duel format genuinely does mitigate it — it is the only one of the three that works with eight players and no coordination.

**Solana primitives.**
  - One match PDA per duel — perfectly sharded state, so the 12M CU/writable-account/block ceiling is never approached and local fee markets keep priority fees near zero on every match
  - USDC vault ATA owned by the match PDA (165 bytes, ~0.00204 SOL rent), closed at settlement so rent is refunded to the players
  - sha256 commit-reveal in two phases: defense = 40 placements x 3 bytes = 120 bytes, assault = 30 entries x 3 bytes = 90 bytes — both far inside the 1,232-byte L1 tx limit, so this ships today without waiting on SIMD-0296
  - Clock sysvar unix_timestamp for the phase deadlines (10 min blitz / 12 h standard) with claim_forfeit for a missed commit or a withheld reveal
  - Jito bundle (5 txs, atomic, same slot) for reveal + resolve + payout + close-vault so a match cannot half-settle and strand escrow

  - Durable nonces for pre-signed reveals, so a standard-mode player can sign their reveal now and have it land 11 hours later without staying online

### Hostile review

- **Verdict:** buildable-with-caveats
- **Solo-dev estimate:** 22 weeks
- **Technical blockers:**
  - Deterministic pathfinding with dynamic rerouters across wasm and native is the entire project and you scheduled it as week one. Concretely: A* tie-breaking depends on binary-heap pop order, which depends on insertion order, which depends on neighbour iteration order; any HashMap anywhere, any f32, any sort that is not total, and wasm and native diverge. You need up to 30 attackers repathing over a 400-tile grid across 600 ticks. Getting this bit-identical is 3-4 weeks with differential fuzzing over millions of random (defense, assault) pairs, and you correctly identify that it will still be the thing that breaks — you just budgeted a fifth of the time it needs. This is also the one bug class where the failure is 'two players saw different outcomes for the same $10 match and both are certain they are right.'
  - Your sandbox performance requirement and your bot defense are the same number pointing in opposite directions. 'Change one spawn tick, re-run instantly' means a 600-tick battle must complete in well under 200ms in wasm — call it 5-20 evaluations/second in the browser, and 100-500/second in native Rust on a laptop. That is exactly the evaluation budget simulated annealing needs. A 30-entry wave list over 6 unit types x 600 spawn ticks x N lanes, against a fully known deterministic defense, with a fast oracle, is a textbook discrete optimization: ~100k evaluations at 2ms = 200 seconds, comfortably inside your 10-minute blitz window and available offline in advance if the defense is revealed before the assault phase clock starts — which it is, by design.
  - The bisection fraud proof is economically dead and should be cut from the roadmap, not just from v1. Bisecting 600 ticks takes ~10 interactive rounds, each an on-chain transaction with a response deadline. With deadlines long enough to be fair (12h) that is 5 days to adjudicate a $10 match; with deadlines short enough to be practical it becomes a liveness DoS against whichever party is asleep. Nobody will ever run it. Say so, and lean on published engine hash plus published reveals as the permanent answer rather than a stopgap.
  - The async score-duel — your flagship cold-start mode and your entire on-ramp — has an off-chain matching step you have not secured. Two strangers each attack the same archived defense and the higher score wins, but WHO decides which two scores are paired, and when? If the operator pairs after scores are known, the operator can construct favourable or unfavourable matchups at will. That is precisely the trust problem you built the commit-reveal architecture to eliminate, reintroduced at the exact point where money changes hands. You need pairing committed before scores are revealed (commit to the pairing rule and a queue position, or pair on entry order with a published deterministic rule), or the whole integrity story has a hole in the middle of the funnel.
  - Rent stranding on abandoned matches. Match PDA (~300 bytes -> 0.00298 SOL) + vault ATA (0.00204 SOL) = 0.005 SOL = $0.40 fronted per duel. If player B joins, commits, and never reveals, someone must pay a signature to run claim_forfeit and close the vault. Nobody pays $0.0004 to reclaim $0.40 for a stranger, so make the forfeit-claimer the rent recipient, or you will accumulate a long tail of dead PDAs holding your players' SOL. This is a small thing that is very annoying to retrofit.
  - Two full editors plus a tick scrubber replay is the heaviest client of the three concepts and you gave it one week. Defense placement with a live 'best-known-attack' preview score means running the solver in the browser continuously while the player drags pieces — that is a background wasm worker with cancellation, incremental re-evaluation, and a UI that stays at 60fps. Realistically 4 weeks, and it is the part that determines whether the game feels good.
- **Economic holes:**
  - The solver does not just eat the assault phase, it eats the defense phase too, and your public archive is what arms it. You claim defense is bot-resistant because it requires modelling the population rather than searching a known target. But you publish every assault ever submitted, free, forever — which turns 'expected shortfall against the attack distribution the field actually produces' into a fully specified objective function with an empirical sample already collected and hosted by you. Maximizing expected survival against N archived assaults is just a more expensive search, not a different kind of problem. And you are charging $7/month for premium access to the solver's training corpus, which a solver operator will pay instantly. Your marketing hook, your retention loop, and your anti-bot argument are the same asset, and it only works for two of the three.
  - $180/day of rake implies ~$2,250/day of wagered volume — 35% of everything Solana Mobile's flagship Seeker Summer produced in July 2026 ($65,000 across 150,000 matches in 10 days, a $0.43 average stake). Your $5/$20 tiers are 12-46x that observed average stake. The demonstrated Solana skill-gaming population wagers in cents, not dollars, and 300 duels/day in blitz mode means 225 tables running continuously around the clock.
  - Your own depthCurve says a strong player clears 80% against a novice 'comfortably, because both phases compound'. At $5 with 8% rake the novice's EV is 0.2 x $9.20 - $5 = -$3.16, or -63% of stake per 25-minute blitz match. That is the extraction rate that matters, not the 8%. And it is worse here than in SALVO because your free sandbox explicitly teaches the player that they went from leaking 40 HP to leaking 900 in four minutes — you have trained them to expect a steep, visible, fully-attributable skill curve, and then you charge them $5 to be on the wrong side of it against someone 80 days further along.
  - When the solver goes public — and someone will post it, because posting it is high-status in exactly the optimization communities you are recruiting from — the assault phase settles overnight and the wagered product becomes 'who ran more iterations'. You name this. What you do not price: that is also the moment your paying humans start unknowingly playing bots in a cash game you marketed as skill-based, which is the precise fact pattern that produced a $719M disgorgement judgment against Papaya Gaming in SDNY in April 2026. That claim was brought by a competitor under the Lanham Act, not by a regulator, and it does not require the operator to be the one running the bots to make 'skill-based, real opponents' a litigable representation.
  - The DRILL subscription is your best revenue line and it is in tension with everything else. $7/month for unlimited archive practice sells to the improving player — but the improving player is the one extracting from novices, so you are being paid twice by the same top decile while the funding source (new losers) churns in three matches. Model it honestly: at 500 MAU and a realistic 3% attach that is $105/month.
  - SOL-denominated rent and faucet against USDC-denominated rake. $0.40/match of rent float plus 0.01 SOL ($0.80) per faucet drip; at SOL $250 both roughly triple while your $5 stake does not. And the faucet is the day-one bot target for all three concepts (see below).
- **Cold start:** Genuinely solved, and it is the strongest single thing about this concept. At 6 concurrent players the async score-duel still functions: both players attack the same archived defense, blind, at whatever hour suits them, and you can seed the archive yourself with 50 hand-built fortresses on day zero so the mode works with literally two humans who never overlap. That is the Skillz model — the one structure in real-money skill gaming that has demonstrably scaled — and you found it independently. The free sandbox works at one player, the 'crack my fortress' share link works at one player, and the leaderboard of who has best solved a given fortress accumulates value with every visitor whether or not they ever pay. None of that is true of SALVO.

The caveats. First, the 2-phase duel — the game you actually designed, the one with the interesting minimax defense layer — needs a live counterparty across two 12-hour phases, so at 6 players it does not exist and your marketing describes a mode nobody can access. New players' first ten paid experiences will all be score duels against a dev-authored archive, which means their impression of REDOUBT is 'a tower-defense puzzle app that charges $2 a go', not 'PvP with real depth'. Second, the async duel's off-chain pairing (see technical blockers) is exercised hardest exactly when the population is smallest, where 'the operator picked my opponent' is least deniable. Third, a thin archive is a solved archive: with 50 defenses and a public solver, every one has a posted optimal assault within a week and the duel degenerates into 'did you read the forum'. Archive freshness has to scale with the player base, which is a chicken-and-egg you should plan for now — dev-authored defenses on a daily rotation, never a static set.
- **Prior art:** The loop is extremely well-proven outside crypto and has never needed a chain. Bloons TD Battles (Ninja Kiwi) has shipped your pitch for over a decade, including a literal Battle Arena mode where players stake medallions on winner-take-all assault games — a shipped, popular, soft-currency version of your exact wagered format, from a studio that decided not to make the stake real money. Clash of Clans and Boom Beach are the async 'attack an archived defense that someone else built' loop at the scale of hundreds of millions of players; it is arguably the single most validated structure in mobile gaming, and neither monetizes by raking player-vs-player stakes. Sanctum, Anomaly and Mindustry cover the build-then-run-the-gauntlet inversion. Mario Maker is the proof that 'design a challenge, publish it, watch strangers try to beat it, archive everything' generates enormous durable engagement — and also the proof that a public archive gets exhaustively solved and speedrun by a small elite within days of posting.

The crypto version essentially does not exist in surviving form, and the closest wagered-skill analogue is Immortal Game: blockchain chess, $15.5M Series A led by The Chernin Game, which shut down its token, NFT marketplace and all crypto prizes in December 2023 with the explicit finding that offering cash with no barrier to entry 'encouraged heavy cheating on the platform and degraded the user experience for legitimate players who want a fair and safe place to play.' Chess is far harder to solve than your assault phase. They had 15 million dollars and concluded the problem was structural.

One genuinely encouraging datapoint: the tower-defense and optimization audience you are targeting (Bloons, Mindustry, Zachtronics, Screeps) is real, large, and — critically — is NOT the same 3.2M daily Solana wallets. You are the only one of the three concepts whose stated audience is outside the crypto-native trading population, which is the only actual escape from the acquisition contradiction. That is worth more than any of your Solana primitives.
- **Suggested fix:** Stop wagering on the assault and start wagering on the defense — with the solver as the referee instead of the threat. Restructure the paid mode so both players submit a DEFENSE on the same committed map, and both defenses are then attacked by the same published, open-source solver plus the same fixed set of N archived assaults; higher survival wins. This is one change and it resolves your biggestRisk by absorbing it. The solver stops being an existential threat and becomes the scoring function — a shared, deterministic, publicly auditable referee that no player has private access to, which means 'who has the better solver' can never be the game. It preserves the phase you correctly identify as the genuinely bot-resistant one (minimax against a distribution) as the entire wagered product. It makes the on-chain integrity story simpler, not harder, because the assault side is now a fixed public input rather than a second commit-reveal round, halving your transaction count and your abandonment surface. Legally it is even tighter than what you have — identical map, identical budget, identical published attacker set, deterministic scoring, no live counterparty and therefore no pairing-variance argument at all. And it improves cold start: defense-vs-the-same-referee is inherently async and scores are directly comparable across all players at all times, so with 6 people online you have a real leaderboard rather than a pairing problem. Keep the assault phase as the free sandbox and the funnel — 'crack my fortress' remains your best share artifact and your best training tool — but do not take money on the half of your game that a weekend of simulated annealing will settle.

## BULWARK  —  44.0/80

*Lens: pve-and-coop*

**Judge means** — depth 7.0 · skill 6.0 · tech 6.0 · chain 4.0 · econ 5.0 · cold start 3.7 · ship 5.3 · originality 7.0

- [advantage-player] Genuinely novel: an adaptive boss that reads your guild's history and counters your habits, which structurally prevents the metagame from ever solving -- but group mortality (one missing caller stalls 39 other people) is a real async-coop killer.
- [solo-shipper] A boss that remembers your guild's tactics is a genuinely fresh self-generating content loop, undercut by needing 40 committed players and the guild-mortality spiral every co-op correspondence game dies of.
- [strategy-player] An adaptive boss that reads your guild's tactics and specifically counters them next time is a genuinely clever director AI; it dies the moment your guild's caller goes quiet, which correspondence games always eventually do.

**Pitch.** Forty players each command one unit defending a station against a machine swarm that remembers every trick you used on it, orders are sealed until they all resolve at once, and the only way through is to actually talk to each other.

**Core loop.** A Siege runs 12 Phases over 4 days; phases resolve every 8 hours (08:00 / 16:00 / 00:00 UTC), so this is asynchronous by construction and needs nobody online at the same time. You command exactly ONE unit from a roster of 12 archetypes (Interceptor, Sapper, Screen, Lance, Kite, Anchor, Relay, Breaker, Warden, Marker, Feeder, Nullifier). Each phase you submit one order — a verb, a target hex on a 19-hex board, and one modifier — as a commit hash. When the phase closes, everyone reveals and the program resolves all 40 orders simultaneously and deterministically. There is zero RNG in resolution. The fog is entirely social: you do not know what your teammates committed unless they told you, and they might be wrong about what they will need to do. The Swarm is a published finite-state machine — 20 states, transition table readable on-chain and printed in the wiki — whose branch each phase is a function of (a) the raid's aggregate action histogram THIS phase and (b) a MEMORY account holding the histograms of your station's last 8 sieges. The swarm is not random, it is a counter-strategy engine pointed at your specific guild's habits. Swarm HP scales at 800 x participants, so a siege runs correctly with 4 players or 40. Time cost is 3-6 minutes per phase of actual decision plus however long you spend arguing in Discord, which is the real session: ~25 minutes of client time and 2 hours of scheming across four days. Missing a reveal defaults your unit to HOLD — suboptimal but never catastrophic. That is deliberate, because the documented killer of correspondence multiplayer (twenty years of webDiplomacy) is one absentee wrecking everyone allied to them.

**Depth curve.** This has the most literal answer to the day-100 question: on day 100 the Swarm has read 100 days of your guild's decisions and is actively punishing them. If your station opens every siege with a Screen wall, the Memory account has recorded that in 8 consecutive histograms and the FSM's opening branch has shifted to the state that shreds Screens. You are not fighting a static boss, you are fighting a compressed record of yourself, so the metagame cannot stabilize — every dominant strategy invalidates itself in about six sieges. That is a self-generating content treadmill produced by ~400 lines of transition table rather than by an art team. Layered on top: 12 archetypes with a genuine counter-web (Nullifier beats Relay beats Lance beats Nullifier, with position mattering), a coordination-depth curve where a new station plays 40 independent units and a veteran station plays four coordinated squads with named plays and a caller, and a difficulty ladder of Tiers 1-9 where Tier 7+ has been cleared by a handful of stations globally. The honest limit: this depth is mostly SOCIAL, so a station that loses its caller drops two tiers overnight. That is true of every raid game ever made and is why guilds are sticky.

**Why on-chain.** Weaker than the other two, and I will say so plainly: a normal server could run this game. The chain adds three specific things and only the third is load-bearing. (1) Commit-reveal on a public ledger lets a player PROVE they committed a covering order before their squadmate asked for it — a promise mechanically impossible to have broken, which changes the texture of raid coordination. A server can do this too, but nobody can verify the server did not peek. (2) The Swarm's FSM and the Memory account are public state, so the developer cannot secretly tune difficulty to sell more repair kits — in a PvE game whose entire value proposition is 'this boss is fair and it is adapting honestly', an auditable adversary is a real consumer feature, and the season's swarm composition is drawn by Switchboard VRF with the proof published before the season opens so even the initial roster was not hand-picked. (3) THE LOAD-BEARING ONE: the Clear Trophy has a hard on-chain supply cap per tier that the issuer cannot raise. A Tier-7 trophy capped at 500 mints ever, in a program whose mint authority is burned, is a credible scarcity commitment no game publisher has ever been able to make — every Web2 'limited' cosmetic has been re-released. Proof-of-achievement is exactly the collectible class where credible supply is the whole value, and it is the mechanism by which players earn real money here without anyone wagering anything.

**Money flow.** IN, three streams, none of which is an entry fee. (1) REFIT KITS, 0.01 SOL (~$0.80): station damage carries between sieges and repairs itself free over 72 hours; a kit repairs instantly. Pure time-versus-money convenience, permanently consumed, the archetypal recurring sink — an active station buys 3-6 per month. (2) COLOURS AND MARKS, 0.03 SOL: combat-neutral cosmetics for units and station banners. (3) STATION CHARTERS, 0.5 SOL (~$40) one-time, hard-capped at 2,000 ever: founding a station with its own persistent Memory and name. OUT: Clear Trophies are minted FREE to every member of a raid that first-clears a tier, are freely tradable, and carry a 5% Token-2022 transfer fee to the developer — this is how players earn, by selling a genuinely scarce proof of a hard achievement, not by beating other players out of money. Charter holders receive 10% of the refit and cosmetic spend of members they personally recruited, paid atomically by the program (the affiliate layer; see legalStructure for its risk). ARITHMETIC: 3,000 monthly actives across 180 active stations. Refit: 1,100 buyers x 4/month x $0.80 = $3,520/mo. Cosmetics: 6% x 3,000 x $2.40 = $432/mo. Charters front-loaded, ~300 sold in year one = $12,000 one-time. Trophy secondary: 500 Tier-7 trophies at ~$45 turning over twice a year = $45,000 volume, $2,250/yr in fees. Less ~$400/mo of affiliate share out, the developer nets ~$3,700/mo recurring. CRITICALLY: no pool anywhere pays returns to depositors. Refit and cosmetic money goes to the developer and to charter holders' own recruiting effort; trophy money moves between a collector and an achiever. There is no deposit-funded payout to decay.

**Legal structure (as generated).** Tier 0 on the gambling axis, cleanly, because BOTH consideration and chance are removed from the reward path. Playing is free, joining a station is free, and every siege at every tier is enterable with a zero-balance wallet — nothing purchasable affects the outcome (refit kits buy time, cosmetics buy nothing, the full roster is available free). Resolution is 100% deterministic: no RNG in combat, no loot roll, no crit, no per-player variance; VRF appears only as a pre-published symmetric season parameter, a fairness feature and not a chance element in any player's outcome. Clear Trophies are earned by skill without payment, so under consideration+chance+prize there is no consideration and no chance — material-element states have nothing to work with, and the UK 'money or money's worth' analysis is irrelevant because there is no gaming (no chance) and no betting (no staker wagering on an uncertain event). The tradable trophy is a receipt for an accomplishment, which is why it must be earned free — the moment a paid entry gates trophy eligibility this becomes a skill contest with a prize and needs the full Tier 1 apparatus (geofencing, age verification, per-state opinions). Do not do that. THE REAL LEGAL EDGE IS SECURITIES, NOT GAMBLING, and it is the Station Charter: a capped, transferable asset paying a revenue share is Howey-adjacent. It survives on the fourth prong — profits derive from the charter holder's OWN efforts (recruiting and running a guild), not the efforts of others, putting it in franchise/affiliate territory rather than investment-contract territory. Hardening required: single level only, never a downline; revenue share limited to members the holder personally recruited; fixed utility price with no representations about appreciation; non-transferable for the first 90 days; no secondary-market promotion by the developer. If a lawyer is uncomfortable the de-risked fallback is nearly free: make the charter non-transferable and pay the 10% as a plain affiliate commission under a click-through agreement, which is ordinary e-commerce. Money transmission is avoided by the program being non-custodial with atomic splits at point of sale.

**Bots.** Botting is close to pointless here, structurally rather than defensively. A bot can submit orders for one unit, but a unit's value comes from being coordinated with 39 others against an adversary that specifically counters repeated patterns — a bot playing a fixed policy is exactly what the Memory account is built to punish, so a station full of bots gets worse every siege while a station of humans gets better. There is no per-account payout to farm: trophies require a first-clear at a tier, are minted only to participants in a clearing raid, and are supply-capped, so a bot farm cannot mint at scale and cannot clear Tier 7 without genuine coordination. Multi-accounting to hold more units in one station is possible but self-defeating — six units means six sets of orders to micro across 12 phases for no additional reward, since trophies scale with the raid, not the wallet. The one real exploit is a griefer joining to feed the Memory account bad histograms, poisoning future sieges; mitigation is that charter holders control membership and ejection, and Memory records are attributed per-unit so a poisoner is visible in the station's own ledger. Sybil resistance on the free path is handled by simply not having a faucet: playing costs nothing, so there is nothing to sybil for.

**First session.** 0:00 — you see a post: 'STATION VIGIL cleared Tier 5 — 41 stations on Earth have done this', with a rendered 12-phase siege replay. Below it an OPEN ROSTER link, because stations recruit publicly. 0:30 — the station page loads: current siege at phase 4 of 12, next resolve in 3h20m, 14 of 20 unit slots filled, JOIN button. No wallet yet. 1:00 — you click Join and get the archetype picker with a 90-second interactive tutorial for each: pick Screen, it explains that Screen absorbs the first two incoming lances on its hex. 2:00 — NOW the wallet prompt, and this is the important moment: joining costs zero. The connect only establishes identity for your order PDA; you need enough SOL for transaction fees, roughly 0.0004 SOL for the whole siege. The page shows an onramp widget AND, more usefully, a 'the station will cover your fees' button — the charter holder can pre-fund a fee vault paying base fees for members, costing about $0.01 per member per siege and removing funding as a first-session blocker entirely. Most new players never touch an onramp; their guild leader spent forty cents on twenty recruits. 3:00 — you are in the station Discord (linked from the page) where somebody types 'new Screen, take hex 7 next phase, the swarm is in Lattice state and it will push left'. 3:30 — you submit your first commit: one transaction, ~5,000 lamports, lands in under a second. 4:00 — you wait 3 hours 20 minutes, which sounds bad and is actually the retention mechanic: you check back three times before the resolve. 3:24:00 — the phase resolves, the replay animates, your Screen ate two lances, the station's damage bar moved. You are now a person who checks a website three times a day.

**Viral mechanic.** Server-first prestige, the oldest and most reliable social engine in cooperative games — 'ONLY 41 STATIONS HAVE CLEARED TIER 5' is publicly verifiable rather than a marketing line, and the Tier-7 trophy's on-chain cap of 500 means the scarcity is checkable by anyone. Every siege generates a shareable animated replay with the station's name on it, and clearing raids post them. The compounding vector is the charter: 2,000 holders whose 10% affiliate share is proportional to how many members they recruit and keep active are 2,000 people with a direct financial reason to run recruitment posts, tutorial threads, and Discord servers — a distributed acquisition team the solo developer does not pay upfront and does not manage. That is the programmatic-cap-table advantage used for the thing it is actually good at. Secondary: because sieges are asynchronous and 8 hours long, the natural clip is a 20-second phase resolution, which reads well on X without sound.

**Four-week MVP.** SHIP: one station, 4-16 players, 5 archetypes (Screen, Lance, Kite, Anchor, Relay), an 8-state Swarm FSM with Memory of the last 4 sieges, 8 phases at 8-hour intervals, 19-hex board, commit-reveal on plain L1, a permissionless resolution crank, CSS hex-grid rendering with unicode glyphs and a phase-by-phase replay scrubber, Discord as the entire social layer. Week 1: FSM design plus a headless simulator to run 5,000 sieges and verify no single strategy dominates and that Memory adaptation actually shifts win rates. Week 2: Anchor program — order PDAs, commit-reveal, resolution crank, station Memory. Week 3: web client, replay renderer, landing layer. Week 4: tutorial, balance from simulator runs, deploy, run siege zero with eight friends. EXPLICITLY CUT: charters and the affiliate share (v2 — the piece needing legal review, must not gate launch), Clear Trophies and Token-2022 (v2, week 6), refit kits and all monetization (v2 — the MVP earns zero dollars on purpose, because the only question week four answers is whether eight people argue about hex 7 in Discord), the ephemeral rollup and Blitz mode (v3), archetypes 6-12, tiers above 3, multi-station support. If it slips, cut to 6 phases and 4 archetypes; the coordination is the game, the content is not.

**Biggest risk.** Group mortality. Cooperative correspondence games die station by station: a guild loses its caller, the Discord goes quiet, phases start defaulting to HOLD, sieges start failing, remaining members leave — and this repo's own wave-1 review already flags exactly this pattern from twenty years of webDiplomacy and Backstabbr, noting that a bond does not fix absenteeism. My mitigations are structural rather than financial: defaulting is never catastrophic (HOLD is a real order, not a forfeit), swarm HP scales with actual participants so a station shrinking from 20 to 6 gets a correspondingly smaller boss rather than an impossible one, and stations can merge. But this is not solved — the honest expectation is that most stations die within two months and survival depends on new stations forming faster than old ones fail, which is why the charter holder's recruiting incentive is the most important economic mechanism in the design and also the one carrying the securities risk. Second risk: this is the weakest whyOnChain of the three. If trophy supply caps, an auditable FSM, and provable commitments turn out not to matter to players, what remains is a free browser game with a wallet attached earning ~$3,700/month that a normal web app could have earned without the complexity.

**Solana primitives.**
  - Commit-reveal via a per-player order PDA holding sha256(order || nonce || phase) — cheap and strong for sealed simultaneous orders, and far cheaper than a VRF callback
  - One MEMORY account per STATION (not per game) — with 2,000 stations there is no hot global account, so the 12M CU per writable account per block ceiling is structurally unreachable; each station's memory is written 3 times per day
  - Phase resolution as a permissionless crank: 40 units x 20 FSM states resolves in ~140k CU, inside the 200,000 CU default instruction limit, touching one station PDA plus 40 order PDAs — note the 64 account-lock limit per transaction caps a single-transaction resolve at ~60 units, which is why roster size is capped at 40 with headroom
  - Jito bundles (up to 5 transactions, atomic, same slot) for the reveal-and-resolve sequence so a phase can never half-apply and leave a station in an undefined state
  - Token-2022 transfer-fee extension for Clear Trophies: 5% of every secondary sale routes to the developer at the token-program level, enforced rather than requested, with mint authority burned at season start so the supply cap is verifiable
  - Switchboard On-Demand VRF drawn once per season for swarm composition, proof published pre-season — symmetric across all stations, so no per-player chance and no gambling coupling
  - Fee arithmetic: 12 phases x 2 transactions (commit + reveal) = 24 signatures per player per siege at 5,000 lamports = 0.00012 SOL (~$0.01) for four days; per-player and per-station PDAs sit in their own local fee markets so priority fees stay near zero
  - MagicBlock Ephemeral Rollup reserved for an optional v3 'Blitz' mode — 40 players, 90-second phases, ~10ms slots, 0 SOL per ER transaction, 0.0003 SOL session plus 0.0001 SOL commit charged once per siege at settlement

### Hostile review

*backfilled 2026-08-21; reviews the raw concept, not the later build spec*

- **Verdict:** buildable-with-caveats
- **Solo-dev estimate:** 5 weeks
- **Degenerate strategy:** Once a station builds even a crude offline replica of the public 20-state Swarm FSM plus its own Memory history, siege planning collapses from 'devise a counter-strategy against an adapting enemy' into 'look up the current state, execute the pre-computed optimal order set.' The coordination problem (can 40 people submit correctly on schedule) remains real and is the design's own acknowledged 'mostly social' depth, but the headline mechanic — an adversary that specifically learns and punishes your guild — becomes a solved table for any station willing to do the one-time lookup-tool work, which the transparency requirement (whyOnChain) makes unavoidable rather than optional.
- **Technical blockers:**
  - Swarm difficulty scales with nominal roster headcount (800 HP x participants), not active headcount. Nothing distinguishes a station with 40 genuinely engaged members from one with 25 active and 15 permanently-HOLD members who never formally left. Since HOLD is deliberately non-catastrophic (the correct anti-webDiplomacy design choice), a station can accumulate dead weight over many sieges without it ever forcing removal, silently overtuning the swarm against the station's real coordination capacity — compounding the design's own stated central risk (group mortality) instead of cushioning it.
  - The Jito-bundle justification in solanaPrimitives ('for the reveal-and-resolve sequence so a phase can never half-apply') doesn't match the described transaction flow: 40 players each sign and submit their own independent reveal transaction over an 8-hour window, not something one submitter composes into a 5-tx bundle, and the final resolve step already touches all 40 order PDAs plus the station PDA atomically in one instruction. Jito isn't doing the job attributed to it here — not fatal, but evidence the transaction-flow model needs another pass before build.
- **Economic holes:**
  - The published, auditable FSM is a double-edged sword the doc only credits the upside of. Publishing all 20 states (for the stated fairness reason) also lets anyone build an offline simulator that, given the public Memory histogram and this phase's aggregate histogram, computes the swarm's exact next branch in advance. Once one such tool exists and circulates (a station needs only one member to build or find it), 'an AI that adapts to your specific guild' degrades into 'the next move is a lookup,' shrinking the concept's headline differentiator to the coordination-execution layer alone.
  - Station Charters are a hard-capped (2,000 ever), fixed-price ($0.5 SOL / ~$40), no-auction mint with no stated per-wallet limit — precisely the asset shape every NFT-mint-sniping bot in the 2021-2022 PFP cycle was built to exploit.
- **Cold start:** Mechanically honest about this and targets it directly (4-16 players, HP scaling by participant count, 'run siege zero with eight friends'), and the siege loop functions fine as one isolated station. What doesn't survive is the business model: the server-first prestige loop ('only 41 stations have cleared Tier 5') needs many competing stations to mean anything, and the Station Charter economics — 2,000 capped charters plus a 10% affiliate-recruiting incentive the design calls its most important economic mechanism — need a multi-station ecosystem to generate any revenue at all. At 8 total players (one station), charter revenue is whatever the founders paid, affiliate income is zero, and cosmetic/refit revenue is a rounding error. Matches the design's own concession that survival depends on new stations forming faster than old ones fail, with no stated mechanism guaranteeing that threshold is crossed.
- **Bot extraction:** The Station Charter mint (fixed $40, capped at 2,000, no stated per-wallet limit, no auction or gradual release) is a textbook mint-sniping target, the single most well-documented extraction pattern in crypto gaming. A scripted buyer submitting the mint transaction repeatedly in the first minutes after launch could plausibly acquire a large fraction of supply — e.g. 200 of 2,000 charters for an $8,000 outlay — before any station has proven it can clear a tier or survive past month two. Since the viral mechanic is built to publicize scarcity and server-firsts, even a modest 2x markup once a handful of stations show real Tier-5+ clears nets a guaranteed ~$8,000 profit extracted from a pre-revenue player base with zero gameplay participation.
- **Prior art:** Dark Forest (zkSNARK Ethereum MMO) is the closest example of a cooperative 'raid boss' emerging in an on-chain strategy game, but that dynamic was emergent and unofficial (players self-organized against a dominant human opponent nicknamed a raid boss), not a developer-authored adaptive-AI encounter with a published FSM and persistent per-guild memory — so BULWARK's specific mechanic has no direct shipped precedent found. Off-chain, guild-raid-against-an-adaptive-boss is a proven, decades-old MMO retention pattern (Guild Wars 2 raids, mobile gacha guild-boss modes), but always on a trusted central server — exactly the trust problem whyOnChain is solving for. The retention mechanic is proven; the on-chain-auditable-adversary angle is not.
- **Suggested fix:** Have the VRF that already seeds season swarm composition also perturb the FSM's transition weights per siege within a published, provable range (e.g. 'this state resolves to branch A with weight 60-80%, drawn and revealed pre-siege') rather than a single deterministic transition per histogram bucket. This preserves the fairness pillar fully (range and draw are both public and provable, so difficulty still can't be secretly softened) while making a static offline lookup table insufficient to fully solve the boss.

## SOUNDING  —  43.7/80

*Lens: unused-primitives*

**Judge means** — depth 7.0 · skill 5.7 · tech 3.3 · chain 7.3 · econ 4.7 · cold start 4.7 · ship 3.3 · originality 7.7

- [advantage-player] Elegant idea -- a map hidden from the operator too -- but it leans on the newest, least-documented piece of Solana infrastructure that exists, and a constraint-solver bot out-probes humans within about two weeks.
- [solo-shipper] Genuinely novel developer-blind exploration economy, but it leans on the single newest, least-documented piece of the entire Solana stack, and the author's own timeline admits 6 weeks, not 4.
- [strategy-player] A Minesweeper-deduction game with an actual market for the information you find is a clever, fresh combination let down by depending on the newest, least-documented piece of Solana infrastructure that exists.

**Pitch.** A hidden world that not even the developer can see, where everybody is exploring the same map, and the thing you actually sell to other players is what you found out.

**Core loop.** Two loops that feed each other.

THE PROBE (10-second beat): The world is a 256x256 hex grid that exists only inside a TEE-backed Private Ephemeral Rollup. Nobody can read it — not other players, not me. You spend 1 ENERGY to probe a hex; the PER returns that hex plus its 6 neighbours, encrypted to your key, in ~10ms (ER slots are ~10ms and ER transactions have no base fee, so probing feels like a native app, not a blockchain). Energy: 120/day cap, regenerating 5/hour, so a session is 20-40 probes.

But probing blind is for beginners, because the world follows PUBLISHED GENERATION RULES: exactly 1,200 deposits exist; no two are within 4 hexes; every deposit is adjacent to at least one river hex; rivers are contiguous chains of length 8-40; deposit density per 32x32 quadrant is 15-22. So a probe is not a scratchcard, it is a Minesweeper clue. Finding a river tells you deposits are near it; finding a deposit tells you 60 hexes around it are empty. Good players build a constraint solver in their head (or on paper) and probe where the information gain is highest. Claiming a deposit costs 3 energy and is first-come — the PER validates the coordinate without revealing anything to a wrong guesser.

THE INTEL MARKET (the reason to log in): You can sell what you know. Publish a SURVEY over an 8x8 region: price it yourself (typical 0.004-0.02 SOL), and any buyer's purchase releases your recorded findings for that region, with the TEE certifying at sale time that your record matches truth — so fraudulent intel is not merely punished, it is impossible to sell. Economic band that makes the market exist: probing an 8x8 region yourself costs ~22 energy (about 4.5 hours of regeneration). Intel is cheap in energy terms and expensive in SOL terms, so the trade is time-for-money, which is a trade real people make.

And intel PERISHES. Every 48 hours a DRIFT event deterministically relocates 5% of unclaimed deposits and shifts river chains by 1-3 hexes. Old surveys decay in value on a known schedule. Information has a half-life, which is the mechanic that makes the market keep clearing instead of collapsing to one perfect map published on day 3.

Session: 25-40 minutes. Burn your energy, update your map, price your surveys, buy two from someone who has been working the northeast.

**Depth curve.** Day 1 you click hexes semi-randomly. Day 100 you are doing three things that did not exist for you on day 1.

(1) CONSTRAINT SOLVING. The generation rules are public, so the map is deductively constrained, and skilled play is inference, not luck. By day 100 you probe the hexes that maximise information gain given everything you know, you recognise the signature of a river chain from two hexes, and you can rule out a 60-hex exclusion zone from one deposit find. This is the Minesweeper expert curve and it is real and steep.

(2) PRICING INFORMATION. This is the unusual one and I have not seen it in a game. By day 100 you have a price history for intel and you have learned things that are genuinely hard: that a survey of a region adjacent to a known river sells for 4x one that is not; that intel decays 30% in value per drift cycle; that you should hold a survey of a dense quadrant for 12 hours to claim it yourself before selling it; that flooding the market with your own surveys collapses your own price. This is a market-making skill and it does not saturate, because the other players keep changing.

(3) REPUTATION AS AN ASSET. Your cumulative verified-accurate survey volume is written to the Solana Attestation Service and persists across seasons. By day 100 a known cartographer's surveys sell at a premium simply because buyers find them first and trust the coverage. That premium is earned over months and cannot be bought, which makes it the durable progression.

The honest limit: the WORLD is not infinitely deep — 1,200 deposits get found, and a six-week season ends. What carries across seasons is your skill, your reputation attestations, and your understanding of worldgen priors (deposit clustering statistics that only emerge from having watched several seasons). A new season is a new map with the same rules, which is the roguelike answer: the content resets, the mastery does not.

**Why on-chain.** The game is trivially buildable on a server — hiding a map from clients is the easiest thing a server does. What is NOT buildable on a server is the MARKET, and the market is the product.

Run this on a server and I, the operator, know the entire map. I am therefore the largest possible insider trader in an economy where information is the only good. I can front-run every survey, seed the market, claim every deposit through an alt, or simply sell the map. No amount of policy language makes 'the guy who can see everything runs the exchange' acceptable to anyone who thinks about it for ten seconds, and the people considering paying 0.02 SOL for a survey will think about it.

A TEE-backed Private Ephemeral Rollup removes the operator from the trust set. The world is generated from a VRF seed injected directly into the enclave; the plaintext map exists only inside attested hardware; probe responses are encrypted to the requesting player's key; and the enclave's attestation is verifiable on L1. I cannot see the map. I can prove I cannot see the map. That is the only configuration in which an operator-run information market is credible.

Commit-reveal does not substitute here, and this is the precise reason a PER is load-bearing rather than decorative. Commit-reveal is cheap and strong for TWO parties who can each hold their own secret. Here there is one shared secret (the world) consulted by hundreds of parties at different times, with partial per-player disclosure and cross-player resale of those disclosures. Someone must hold the preimage and answer queries. If that someone is a person, they are the insider. The TEE is the only way to make it nobody.

Second thing that breaks on a server: instant global settlement of thousands of sub-cent-to-few-cent information sales between strangers with no accounts, no chargebacks, and no payment processor that would ever approve this business.

**Money flow.** Deliberately the simplest of the three, because the legal structure demanded it.

INFLOW 1 — SEASON PASS. 0.2 SOL (~$16) for a six-week season. Unlimited probing within the energy budget. This is a subscription to a game, not a payment per reveal, and that distinction is the entire legal design (see below). 100% to the house. 500 passholders = 100 SOL/season (~$8,000 per six weeks, ~$5,300/month).

INFLOW 2 — INTEL MARKET RAKE. 10% of every survey sale. Seller keeps 90%, instantly, in SOL. If 500 players each buy 25 surveys a season at an average 0.008 SOL, that is 100 SOL of volume, 10 SOL to the house and 90 SOL circulating between players. The rake is small on purpose: the market's job is retention, not revenue.

OUTFLOW: there is none from the house. No purse, no prizes, no rewards, no emissions, no token. Money enters as a subscription and as player-to-player payment for a produced good.

WHERE PLAYER EARNINGS COME FROM: exclusively other players buying knowledge those players chose to buy. A dedicated cartographer working the market well might clear 3-8 SOL a season (~$240-640) — real money, roughly a good freelance week, and it comes from having produced something (coverage of a region) that someone else valued more than their own time. That is a labour market, not a lottery, and it is the only honest 'earn' in these three concepts.

SOLO-DEV VIABILITY: ~$5,300/month at 500 passholders, ~$1,000/month at 100. The revenue does not depend on trading volume, speculation, a token price, or new entrants funding old ones. If the population halves, revenue halves and nothing breaks — no obligation goes unmet, because I never took on an obligation. The failure mode is 'small business', not 'collapse'.

SINK NOTE: energy is the real economy. It is not purchasable at any price. There is no way to convert money into more probes, which means the wealthy cannot outproduce the diligent, and it means the game cannot be pay-to-win even accidentally.

**Legal structure (as generated).** Tier 0. No prize of value is awarded by the operator, so there is no gambling analysis to run at all — and I made specific, costly design choices to keep it there.

The design started somewhere worse. The obvious version charges per probe: pay 0.001 SOL, maybe uncover something worth 0.05. That is consideration + chance + prize, dressed as exploration, and it is functionally a dig-for-treasure game. Regulators look at what a product functionally IS, not at the taxonomy — that is the whole lesson of PrizePicks paying ~$15M to New York over a product it insisted was fantasy sports. So the per-probe charge is gone.

What replaced it: a FLAT SEASON PASS with a non-purchasable energy budget. You buy access to a game for six weeks, exactly as you buy a game. There is no per-reveal transaction, no draw, no pull, and no way to spend more money to get more reveals. Removing the ability to pay for energy costs me obvious revenue and I am giving it up on purpose, because it is the difference between a subscription and a slot machine.

The three legs: CONSIDERATION exists (the season pass). CHANCE is heavily de-materialised — everybody explores the SAME world (symmetric, not per-player draws), the generation rules are fully published so the map is deductively constrained rather than blindly guessed, and the drift schedule is deterministic and known. PRIZE is absent: deposits award ranking points and a SOULBOUND claim certificate, minted non-transferable via Bubblegum V2. There is nothing to cash out. No purse, no payout, no prize pool, at any tier. With prize removed the analysis ends, in every state, under every chance test.

WHAT ABOUT PLAYERS EARNING? They earn by selling surveys to other players. That is a marketplace for user-produced content, structurally the same as selling a map pack or a strategy guide. The operator awards nothing. What players sell is a work product, and its value comes from labour (energy spent, deduction applied), not from a random outcome. Note the ordering matters: you cannot sell intel you did not produce, and producing it costs time you cannot buy.

UK: nothing here is acquired by chance in exchange for payment. The season pass buys access, surveys are bought at a stated price for stated coverage, and claims are non-transferable so they are not money's worth. This sits well clear of the loot-box question.

RESIDUAL EXPOSURE, honestly: money transmission. I operate a marketplace where users pay users and I take 10%. The 10% is a platform fee on a peer-to-peer settlement I never custody — funds move wallet-to-wallet in one atomic transaction and I never hold them — which is the standard non-custodial posture, but it is the item I would put in front of counsel first. Second: the intel market must not be gameable into a de facto prize pool (e.g. me buying surveys from top players). I do not participate in the market at all, ever, and that is enforced by the program not by policy — the house wallet is rejected as a buyer at the instruction level.

**Bots.** Bots are strong here and I want to be precise about where.

WHAT A BOT DOES DAY ONE: writes a constraint solver. The generation rules are public and the map is a constraint-satisfaction problem, so a solver picks maximum-information-gain probes better than a human by roughly week two. That is real, and unlike Lockmaker there is money on the other side of it, because good probing produces sellable intel.

WHAT STOPS IT: energy. 120 probes/day, regenerating 5/hour, non-purchasable at any price. A bot with a perfect solver and a human with a decent one have exactly the same number of probes. Compute is unlimited; the input to compute is rationed. This is the single most important anti-bot decision in the design and it is why energy is not for sale — the moment energy is purchasable, the bot with capital outproduces everyone and the intel market becomes a machine for converting capital into other players' money.

MULTI-ACCOUNTING: the actual threat. Ten season passes at 0.2 SOL = 2 SOL for 1,200 probes/day, feeding one solver, dumping intel into the market. That works, and it is a business. Three mitigations: (1) each pass is a real 0.2 SOL cost, so the sybil must clear ~$16/account/season in intel sales, which caps the profitable fleet size to something small in a market with a few hundred buyers; (2) listing surveys above a volume threshold requires a proof-of-personhood attestation via the Solana Attestation Service, read from a third-party issuer — a bot can probe with 10 wallets but can only SELL through one identity, which is exactly the choke point that matters; (3) reputation is per-identity and compounds, so a fleet of fresh anonymous sellers competes against established cartographers on the one axis they cannot fake.

WHY THE DAMAGE IS BOUNDED ANYWAY: there is no faucet. The house pays nothing out. A bot's maximum extraction is what other players voluntarily pay it for genuinely accurate, genuinely useful intel — which means a maximally successful bot is a supplier that makes the map cheaper for everyone. That is a competitor, not a parasite. Compare this to a wagering game, where a bot's profit is other players' losses. Removing the prize pool removed the botting incentive along with it.

And honestly: I would let bots probe openly, and expect the strongest human players to write them. The thing I gate is selling, because that is where identity actually matters.

**First session.** Minute 0. Link in a Discord: 'SOUNDING · Season 1 · 1,200 deposits · 341 found · Day 9 of 42'. The landing page is the SHARED PUBLIC MAP — a 256x256 CSS grid where every hex any player has publicly revealed is coloured and everything else is black. On day 9 it is ~85% black with bright veins where people have been working. It looks like something being uncovered, because it is. No art assets, just coloured divs and monospace type.

Minute 0-3. A free TUTORIAL WORLD, no wallet: a 32x32 practice map with 20 deposits and the same generation rules. You probe, you get clues, you deduce, you find three deposits. Roughly three minutes and you understand that this is deduction, not luck. This is the pitch and it costs nothing to receive.

Minute 3. 'Join Season 1.' Passkey wallet (Face ID, no seed phrase). Funding: 0.2 SOL (~$16) — the largest up-front ask of the three concepts and the honest friction point. Two paths: existing Solana wallet (~30 seconds, which is most of week one's realistic audience) or card on-ramp (~2 minutes, poor conversion). There is no free tier past the tutorial and I am accepting that: a season pass is the legal structure, and a free tier full of alts would break the anti-sybil design that the pass fee provides.

Minute 4-6. First real session. 120 energy waiting. The client asks a question a normal game would not: 'Where do you want to start?' with three suggested frontiers based on where the public map has open edges. You probe 15 hexes near an unexplored river mouth, hit a river chain, follow it, and find your first deposit at probe 23. The claim resolves in ~10ms in the PER and the hex goes bright on the public map with your name on it. That moment — 'the whole map now shows a thing I found' — is the retention hook, and it happens in the first six minutes.

Minute 8. You burn the rest of your energy. Now you have 90 hexes of private knowledge about a quadrant nobody else has touched.

Minute 12. The market page. You see 40 surveys for sale from other players, priced 0.003-0.03 SOL, sorted by region and freshness with a visible decay countdown to the next drift. You buy one for 0.006 SOL covering the region next to yours; it decrypts instantly and adds 64 hexes to your map. Then the realisation the whole game is built to produce: you have something to sell too. You list your quadrant at 0.01 SOL.

Minute 20. You close the tab. Energy regenerates at 5/hour, so you come back in four hours or tomorrow — and when you do, the first thing you check is whether anyone bought your survey. Someone did. 0.009 SOL landed in your wallet while you were asleep, from a stranger, for something you figured out.

**Viral mechanic.** (1) THE PUBLIC MAP AS A LIVING ARTEFACT. One image, updated continuously, showing a world being uncovered by named people. It is screenshottable on any day of a season and it looks different every day. Day 3 it is nearly black; day 40 it is a bright, weird, organic shape defined entirely by where players chose to look. That progression is a free, self-generating content stream, and the shape itself is legible evidence of collective effort in a way a leaderboard never is.

(2) THE FIRST-CLAIM FEED. Every deposit claim posts to a public feed with the claimant's name and the hex. 1,200 of these per season, each one a small, real, permanent 'I found this'. Named discovery is the oldest viral mechanic in exploration games and it costs nothing to build.

(3) CARTOGRAPHER REPUTATION AS A PUBLIC CV. SAS attestations are readable by anyone, persist across seasons, and mean something specific (verified-accurate coverage). 'Season 1 top cartographer' is a durable, checkable credential, and people share credentials.

**Biggest risk.** Single-vendor dependency on the newest, least-documented piece of infrastructure in the entire Solana stack.

The TEE-backed Private Ephemeral Rollup is what makes this concept possible, and it is one region operated by one company whose ER trust model — fraud proofs, data availability, validator-failure and undelegation recovery — is not documented publicly and is not answered in their FAQ. I would be putting a live economy behind hardware attestation I cannot independently audit, from a vendor with no published failure-recovery story, in a product whose entire pitch is 'you do not have to trust the operator'. If the TEE region has an outage, the world is unreadable and the game stops — not degrades, stops. There is no fallback mode, because the fallback is a trusted server and a trusted server invalidates the premise.

Second risk, and it is a design risk rather than an infrastructure one: information markets are famously illiquid. The buyer's problem is that they cannot evaluate intel before buying (I solve honesty via the TEE, but not usefulness — a perfectly accurate survey of an empty region is worthless and looks identical to a valuable one before purchase). If buyers get burned on a few worthless-but-accurate surveys, they stop buying, and the market — which is the retention engine and half the reason the concept exists — thins out to nothing. Partial mitigations exist (sellers must disclose deposit counts, which the TEE certifies; reputation compounds; prices sort by region) but 'the market might just not clear' is a real possibility and I would know within one season.

Third: the 0.2 SOL up-front pass is the highest barrier of the three concepts, gating a game whose fun is not provable until after payment. The tutorial world is the mitigation and it may not be enough.

**Solana primitives.**
  - MagicBlock Private Ephemeral Rollup (TEE-backed) — load-bearing, the whole design. World state lives in delegated accounts inside the TEE region; ~10ms slots make probing feel instant; ER transactions carry no base fee so a 40-probe session costs nothing per action; 0.0003 SOL per ER session and 0.0001 SOL per L1 commit means a season of settlement costs a player pennies. The 64KB ER transaction limit (vs 1,232 bytes on L1) is what lets a survey payload — 64 hexes of certified content — move in one transaction instead of being chunked.
  - Solana Attestation Service — cartographer reputation. Cumulative verified-accurate survey volume and total claims are written as attestations, persist across seasons, and are readable by any client. Also used defensively: high-tier survey listings require a proof-of-personhood attestation from a third-party issuer (Civic et al.), so I consume identity infrastructure rather than build it.
  - Switchboard On-Demand VRF — generates the world seed, injected into the enclave. The point is not that the world is random, it is that no human ever saw the seed.
  - Compressed NFTs (Bubblegum V2) — survey records and claim certificates are mass-issued cNFTs (hundreds of thousands per season at fractions of a cent). Claims are minted SOULBOUND (a Bubblegum V2 feature) — deliberately non-transferable, which is a legal decision as much as a design one.
  - L1 settlement with per-player sharding — claims, energy, and market ledgers commit to per-player PDAs, never one global account, staying clear of the 12M CU per-writable-account per-block ceiling and keeping priority fees near zero since fee markets are local to writable accounts.
  - Explicitly NOT used: no transfer hooks, no token, no purse, no prize pool, no per-draw payments.

*Lens: wagered-strategy-a*

- [advantage-player] The one genuinely pro-resistant property in this whole set: an equilibrium bot only wins ~50% and still loses to the 8% rake, so solving the game doesn't make it profitable to farm -- but 1v1 liquidity at 8 players is a real cold-start problem.

**Pitch.** A six-minute wargame duel where both players secretly write orders for three columns each turn, the map is a perfect mirror so neither side gets a lucky start, and the winner takes the pot.

**Core loop.** 9x9 grid, 180-degree rotationally mirrored. Each side: 3 columns (HP 40) plus a base (HP 100), 10 fuel cap. Every turn a 15-second clock runs and you secretly assign each of your 3 columns one order: MOVE (1 fuel, one tile), FIRE (2 fuel, 12 damage at range 2, 20 at range 1), FORTIFY (0 fuel, incoming halved this turn), SUPPLY (+3 fuel, column cannot act). You submit a hash; when both hashes land the clock stops and both reveal. Resolution is fixed: movement first (head-on swaps bounce, same-tile entries both bounce), then all fire simultaneously with no initiative order. 20 turns hard cap; base at 0 ends it; at turn 20 higher base HP wins, tie-broken by total damage then fuel. The 30-second decision is: 'he has 3 fuel and a column adjacent to my base — if I FORTIFY I lose tempo, if I FIRE and he MOVEd I hit air, if I SUPPLY I can double-fire next turn.' Live mode 5-7 minutes; postal mode is the identical game with a 12-hour clock per turn. Stakes $1 / $5 / $25 USDC, 8% rake.

**Depth curve.** Honest answer first: this has the weakest day-100 curve of the three, because simultaneous-move games drift toward mixed-strategy equilibria at the very top and a 9x9 board with 3 units is small enough that a dedicated community could map most openings within a year. What keeps it alive to day 100 is (a) fuel, an accumulating resource that turns each turn into a state-dependent matrix game rather than repeated rock-paper-scissors — a bluff that costs 2 fuel is not free, so the equilibrium is never uniform; (b) yomi layering — you are modelling a specific human, and against a known opponent exploitative play beats equilibrium play, which is why poker stayed interesting; (c) a rotating pool of 8 mirrored map families (choke, open, split-lane, etc.) selected per match, so opening theory has to be learned eight times over. Concretely: by day 10 you know that double-FIRE on turn 3 loses to a FORTIFY read. By day 100 you know which players over-FORTIFY when behind on fuel, and you are farming that. A strong player should hit 80%+ against a novice by turn 8 purely through fuel-tempo management, which is deterministic and unforgiving.

**Why on-chain.** Almost none of the gameplay needs a chain and I will not pretend otherwise — a Node server could run this loop fine. Three things break without it. First, escrow between two anonymous strangers with no signup: the program PDA holds both USDC entries and pays out atomically, so I never custody funds and neither player can rug the other mid-match. Second, the seed commitment: the mirrored map is derived from hash(match_pda, commit_a, commit_b), published before either player sees the board, so nobody has to trust that I did not hand-pick a map. Third, and this is the actual business reason: global permissionless settlement at $0.0004 a signature with no bank, no payout rails, no chargebacks, no 30-day hold. A solo dev cannot build Skillz's payments stack; they can deploy an Anchor program that does the same job in 400 lines.

**Money flow.** Value enters as USDC deposited by players from their own wallets; there is no token, no mint, no emission. A $5 table: $10 in, 8% rake = $0.80 to the treasury, $9.20 to the winner. On-chain cost per live match: 0.0003 SOL session + 0.0001 SOL commit + ~4 L1 signatures at 5,000 lamports = ~0.0007 SOL, about $0.056 at SOL $80 — 7% of the rake. Postal mode is pure L1: ~44 signatures across both players = 0.00022 SOL, paid by the players themselves in SOL, not out of the rake. Net dev take ~$0.74 per $5 match; 150 matches/day = $111/day. The cost is targeting: with 40 build points you will have 10-20 firing towers; each tower must select a target each tick. 20 towers x 16 attackers x 192 ticks = 61,440 target evaluations, each a squared-distance computation plus range compare plus loop overhead — on SBF, with no SIMD and ~1 CU per instruction, conservatively 30-80 CU each. That is 1.8M-4.9M CU for targeting alone, before movement, damage application, cooldowns and Anchor deserialization. Your 1.2M number is off by 3-4x and the 1.4M per-transaction ceiling is hard. Profile this in week one, exactly as you said, and expect 3-5M.
  - BUT THE FALLBACK YOU CHOSE IS THE WRONG ONE, AND THAT IS THE GOOD NEWS. Optimistic settlement with 0.02 SOL bonds and a 24h challenge window drags in a dispute subsystem, a challenge UI, a griefing analysis and a support burden a solo dev cannot carry in four weeks. The boring answer works: CHUNK THE SIMULATION ACROSS MULTIPLE TRANSACTIONS with a checkpoint in the Match PDA. Store tick index, 16 attacker states (position, hp, type = ~8 bytes each = 128 B) and tower cooldowns (~80 B) in a 512-byte Match PDA and resume. Four transactions gives you 5.6M CU of budget, costs 4 signatures = 20,000 lamports = $0.0016 at SOL $80, and the rent is (512+128) x 6,960 = 0.00445 SOL, fully refunded on close. This deletes your stated biggest risk entirely for about two days of work. Delete the challenge path from the roadmap.
  - FORTS ARE PUBLIC AND SUBMITTED ASYNCHRONOUSLY — THAT IS A COMMIT-REVEAL HOLE YOU DID NOT PLUG. 'Before round 1 you design ONE fort... that fort is fully public.' Entrant #1 submits, entrant #32 reads all 31 forts and builds the hard counter with full knowledge of the field. In a Swiss where your fort is locked for all five rounds, last-mover advantage in fort submission is enormous and monotone in registration time. You need a commit-reveal on fort submission (hash at entry, reveal at lock) or a simultaneous reveal at tournament start. This is 1 day of work and it is currently absent.
  - DETERMINISTIC SWISS PAIRING IS STEERABLE, NOT COLLUSION-PROOF. 'Score, then rating, then pubkey' is fully computable from public state before each round. A colluder with two entries can therefore compute exactly which score bracket lands his alt as his opponent, and deliberately win or dump round 2 to arrive there in round 3. Deterministic pairing does not prevent steering, it PERFECTS it — it removes the uncertainty that normally makes Swiss manipulation risky. Also, 'then pubkey' is a grindable tiebreak: vanity keygen at ~10^6 keys/sec means a competitor can mine a pubkey that wins or loses every tiebreak he wants. Neither is fatal — flat payouts blunt the value — but both claims in your botStory are affirmatively wrong.
  - UPGRADE AUTHORITY VOIDS THE 'NO OPERATOR WITHDRAWAL AUTHORITY' CLAIM, same as the others: a program deployed with default settings can be replaced by its upgrade authority and every escrow PDA drained. Squads multisig with timelock, or --final. State which, in the terms.
  - LANDING LAYER: five rounds x 48h windows is forgiving, so this is your easiest case — but a chunked simulation means 4 sequential transactions that must all land, and a partial simulation leaves a Match PDA in a mid-tick state. Make the chunks idempotent and resumable by anyone (permissionless 'continue simulation' instruction), or the first RPC hiccup strands a match and you settle it by hand. Budget 1 week for landing plus resumability.
  - RENT ARITHMETIC OMITS THE 128-BYTE ACCOUNT HEADER THROUGHOUT. Match PDA at 256 B is (256+128) x 6,960 = 0.00267 SOL, not 0.00178. Fort PDA at 192 B is (192+128) x 6,960 = 0.00223 SOL. Tournament PDA at 1 KB is (1024+128) x 6,960 = 0.00802 SOL. Every figure in the doc is low by roughly the same factor. All refundable; still, fix the numbers before anyone else checks them.
- **Economic holes:**
  - THE GUARANTEED PRIZE POOL IS A FARMABLE ARBITRAGE AND IT IS THE BIGGEST HOLE IN ANY OF THE THREE DESIGNS. You promise 2.944 SOL of prizes across 12 places, guaranteed with dev overlay, and you run tournaments from 8 entrants. Eight entries pay 0.8 SOL. Twelve places pay 2.944 SOL. A single actor registers 8 accounts for 0.8 SOL, is guaranteed every prize position that exists, and collects 2.944 SOL. NET +2.144 SOL (~$171) PER TOURNAMENT, RISK-FREE, REPEATABLE, AND IT REQUIRES ZERO GAME SKILL. Your entire anti-sybil argument — 'buying four extra entries costs 0.4 SOL and cannot feed the entry you care about' — assumes the pool is funded by entries. You explicitly promised it is not. The sybil does not need to feed one entry; he needs the field to be smaller than the payout table, which you guaranteed it can be. On day one, the person who bots this is not a simulator-solver: it is somebody with a script that watches for under-filled tournaments and fills them with his own wallets. He extracts your overlay, which is your personal money, and he scales it with your failure to grow.
  - EVEN WITHOUT SYBILS, THE OVERLAY IS AN UNCAPPED SHORT OPTION ON YOUR OWN COLD START. At 8 honest entrants you personally pay 2.144 SOL (~$171) per tournament and collect 0.064 SOL in rake. At 16 entrants you pay ~1.3 SOL. The overlay is largest exactly when you have the fewest players and the least money, which is month one. You have written a product whose loss function is maximized at launch.
  - REVENUE DOES NOT CLEAR THE COST OF THE COMPLIANCE SURFACE. 8% of 3.2 SOL = 0.256 SOL = $20 per 32-player tournament. Your own optimistic case — four concurrent tournaments at 0.25 SOL entry — is ~$600/month. An Anchor escrow audit is $15-40k. Age/identity verification (a legal requirement of the Tier 1 posture) costs $0.50-2.00 per verified user via any real KYC vendor, i.e. $16-64 per 32-player tournament against $20 of rake. YOUR AGE-GATE COSTS MORE THAN YOUR RAKE. That single line invalidates the 0.1 SOL entry tier as a business; the minimum viable staked entry is probably 0.5-1.0 SOL, which shrinks the addressable player set further.
  - ENGAGEMENT PER DOLLAR IS THIN, AND YOUR OWN BOT STORY CONCEDES IT. You correctly predict that solvers will find near-optimal assaults within a month and that the game 'collapses gracefully' into a fort-design contest. Follow that through: the human contribution per 10-day tournament becomes ~30 minutes of fort design, and the five assault rounds become script submissions. That is a form you fill out once every ten days. Games monetize attention; you have designed for 30 minutes of attention per $8 of entry per 10 days. The rake per player-hour is fine; the rake per player-CALENDAR-DAY, which is what determines whether anyone remembers you exist, is near zero.
  - FLAT PAYOUTS ACROSS 12 OF 32 (37.5% of the field paid, top prize 5x entry) means the median good player roughly breaks even after rake and the top player earns $40 for ten days. Nobody optimizes hard for $40. The flat curve is correct anti-sybil design and it is simultaneously an anti-motivation design; you cannot have both at this entry price.
- **Cold start:** Six players is a hard zero: tournaments fire at 8, so at 6 concurrent players nothing runs, ever, including the free tier — the free tournaments have the same 8-entrant floor. That is a cliff, not a slope, and it is the worst cold-start structure of the three concepts. Below the threshold the app has literally nothing to display. Above it, at say 10 entrants, the guaranteed 12-place payout table pays MORE POSITIONS THAN THERE ARE PLAYERS, so every entrant profits, the dev funds the difference, and the tournament is a subsidy rather than a contest. There is a narrow, useful mitigation you already half-have and should lean on hard: the 'crack this' link is a zero-friction, no-wallet, wasm-only single-player challenge, and it works on people who will never touch crypto. Ship that FIRST, as a standalone toy with no chain at all, and only add tournaments once one fort has been attacked a thousand times by strangers. Lower the tournament floor to 4 with a 4-place payout table, drop the guarantee, and let the format scale up rather than requiring 8 to exist.
- **Prior art:** The mechanic is proven — that is the good news and you should say it out loud. Asynchronous raiding of another player's locked, public, deterministic base is CLASH OF CLANS, a game that grossed north of $10B, and Redoubt is essentially Clash's raid layer with the base-building metagame replaced by a single locked fort and the loot replaced by a Swiss score. Tower-offense as a genre (Anomaly: Warzone Earth) and competitive TD (Bloons TD Battles, still live and monetizing a decade on) both demonstrate durable demand. The quartermaster constraint — each attacker type usable at most twice across five rounds — is the genuinely novel piece and it is the best single mechanic across all three of these designs, because it converts a solvable search problem into decision-under-uncertainty about opponents you have not seen, which is exactly the class of problem solvers handle worst.

The crypto record for this genre is uniformly grim, and it is the version you must not resemble. Goombles, a mech-NFT tower defense, shipped in 2024 to 339 installs and the team halted development after two years. Crypto: Against All Odds — Tower Defense was pulled from Steam in March 2026. Ragnarok: Monster World ended service in December. Pirate Nation's developer Proof of Play shut it down citing plainly that too few people played to be financially viable. The common thread in every one of those is an asset economy: NFTs, towers you buy, tokens you farm. Redoubt has none of that — forts are non-transferable PDA data, prizes are SOL, there is no mint — which is precisely why it is not on that list, and you should be explicit that the deaths above were caused by the thing you deliberately omitted.

On the solver question the relevant prior art is Battlecode, Halite (Two Sigma) and Screeps: all deterministic-simulator competitive programming leagues, all with genuinely engaged communities, ALL FREE TO ENTER WITH SPONSOR-FUNDED PRIZES. Nobody has made paid entry work in that shape. The business-model prior art is Skillz — async skill tournaments with entry fees and rake, claimed legal in 38+ states — which is the right template and also a company whose most recent headline is a $719M judgment about bots in skill-based contests.
- **Suggested fix:** REPLACE THE GUARANTEED POOL WITH A PUBLISHED PERCENTAGE TABLE ON ACTUAL ENTRIES, PLUS A MINIMUM-ENTRANT CANCEL-AND-REFUND. Concretely: prizes are a fixed, published percentage split of (total entries minus 8% rake), the payout table scales with field size (4-8 entrants pays 2 places, 9-16 pays 4, 17-32 pays 8-12), and if the field is below 4 at lock time the tournament cancels and every entry and every lamport of rent is refunded automatically by the program. This single change (a) closes the +2.144 SOL sybil arbitrage completely, because a sybil filling an empty tournament now just pays himself back minus your rake — a strictly negative EV loop; (b) removes an unbounded personal liability that peaks exactly when you have no money; (c) keeps the legal posture intact, because a published pari-mutuel table with a stated minimum is the structure every regulated poker room, esports circuit and skill-gaming operator uses and is far easier to defend than a promise you cannot fund; (d) lets you drop the entrant floor from 8 to 4, which is the difference between 'nothing ever runs' and 'something runs tonight' during month one. Do this before you write the escrow code, because the payout table shape determines the account layout. Then spend the two days you saved on chunked multi-transaction simulation with a resumable checkpoint in the Match PDA, and delete the optimistic-settlement challenge subsystem from the plan entirely — those two changes together remove both of your stated biggest risks and cost less than a week.
